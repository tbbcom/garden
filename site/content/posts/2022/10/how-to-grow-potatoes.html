Quick answer

Plant seed potatoes 2–4 weeks before your last spring frost, once the soil is at least 45°F. Set pieces 4 inches deep and 10–12 inches apart in full sun, hill soil over the stems as they grow, and water about 1–2 inches a week. Dig new potatoes 2–3 weeks after flowering, or wait until the vines die back for full-size potatoes to store, usually 70–120 days after planting depending on the variety.

Potatoes are one of the most rewarding crops for beginners: a few pounds of seed potatoes can fill a basket by late summer. The method is simple, but a few details decide whether you get a handful of marbles or a big harvest: timing, depth, hilling and even watering. This guide walks through each step, from buying seed potatoes to curing and storing your crop.

Key takeaways
  • When: 2–4 weeks before the last spring frost, soil 45°F or warmer.
  • Depth and spacing: 4 inches deep, 10–12 inches apart, rows 30–36 inches apart.
  • Hilling: mound soil around stems when plants are 6–8 inches tall, then again 2–3 weeks later.
  • Water: 1–2 inches a week, most important while plants flower and tubers form.
  • Harvest: new potatoes after flowering; storage potatoes 2–3 weeks after the vines die.

When should you plant potatoes?

Potatoes are a cool-season crop. Tubers form best when the soil stays between about 60 and 70°F, and growth slows in hot summer weather. Plant 2–4 weeks before your average last spring frost, as soon as the soil can be worked and has warmed to at least 45°F. Light frost may nip early shoots, but the plants regrow from below ground.

In mild-winter areas such as the Gulf Coast, Southern California and other zone 9–10 gardens, plant in late winter, and you can also grow a fall crop planted in late summer. In the tropics, grow potatoes in the coolest months or at higher elevation.

How do you choose and prepare seed potatoes?

  • Buy certified seed potatoes from a garden center or seed company. Grocery-store potatoes are often treated to stop sprouting and can carry disease.
  • Pick the type: early varieties (about 70–90 days, for new potatoes and summer eating), mid-season, or late varieties (about 100–130 days, best for winter storage).
  • Chit (pre-sprout) them for 2–4 weeks before planting: set them in a single layer, eyes up, in a bright spot at about 50–60°F. Short, sturdy green sprouts give the crop a head start.
  • Cut large potatoes into pieces about the size of a hen's egg (1½–2 oz), each with at least 1–2 eyes. Let cut pieces dry for 1–2 days so the cut surface heals. Plant small seed potatoes whole.

Where should you plant potatoes?

Choose a spot with full sun (6–8 hours a day) and loose, well-drained soil. Potatoes prefer slightly acidic soil, about pH 5.0 to 6.5. Lower pH also helps prevent potato scab, so don't add lime unless a soil test calls for it.

  • Work in compost before planting. Avoid fresh manure, which encourages scab; use only well-rotted manure.
  • Rotate: don't plant where potatoes, tomatoes, peppers or eggplant grew in the last 3 years. They share diseases such as early and late blight.
  • Heavy clay? Grow in raised beds, deep containers or grow bags so tubers have loose soil to expand into.

How do you plant potatoes step by step?

  1. Dig a trench about 6 inches deep. Space rows 30–36 inches apart, or 24 inches in a raised bed.
  2. Add a little compost or a balanced fertilizer to the bottom of the trench, following the label rate, and mix it into the soil so it doesn't touch the seed pieces directly.
  3. Set seed pieces cut side down, sprouts up, 10–12 inches apart.
  4. Cover with 3–4 inches of soil, leaving the trench partly open so you can fill it in as you hill.
  5. Water once to settle the soil, then water only if the soil dries out before shoots appear.

A 10-foot row holds about 10–12 seed pieces, which is roughly 2–3 lb of seed potatoes. Need to fill a raised bed? The raised bed soil calculator tells you how much soil and compost to buy.

How and when do you hill potatoes?

New potatoes form on underground stems above the seed piece, so burying the stems gives the plant more room to make tubers and keeps them out of the light. Exposed potatoes turn green and develop solanine, a bitter compound that shouldn't be eaten.

  1. When plants are 6–8 inches tall, draw soil up around the stems with a hoe, leaving the top 2–3 inches of leaves exposed.
  2. Repeat 2–3 weeks later, building a ridge about 8–12 inches high.
  3. Instead of soil, you can use a thick layer of straw. It also keeps the soil cool and moist.

How much water do potatoes need?

Give potatoes about 1–2 inches of water a week from rain plus watering, soaking the soil deeply rather than sprinkling daily. Even moisture matters most from flowering onward, when tubers are forming: dry spells followed by heavy watering cause cracked, knobby or hollow potatoes. Water at the soil rather than over the leaves to reduce blight. Once the foliage starts to yellow, cut back on watering so the skins can firm up.

How do you keep potato plants healthy?

  • Weed early while plants are small; hilling buries most weeds.
  • Colorado potato beetles: check the undersides of leaves for orange egg clusters and striped beetles, and remove them by hand into soapy water.
  • Blight: brown spots with yellow halos (early blight) or dark, greasy patches that spread fast in wet weather (late blight). Remove affected leaves, keep foliage dry and rotate beds next year.
  • Feeding: compost at planting is usually enough. Too much nitrogen gives lush leaves and few tubers.

When and how do you harvest potatoes?

HarvestWhenBest for
New potatoes2–3 weeks after plants flower (about 60–70 days for early varieties)Eating fresh within a few days
Main crop2–3 weeks after the vines yellow and die back (about 90–130 days)Storage through winter
  1. Test-dig one plant first. Storage potatoes are ready when the skin doesn't rub off easily with your thumb.
  2. Dig on a dry day. Start 12–18 inches from the stem with a garden fork and lift the whole root mass to avoid spearing tubers.
  3. Cure unwashed potatoes for 1–2 weeks in a dark, humid place at about 50–60°F so small wounds heal and the skins toughen.
  4. Store in the dark at about 40–45°F with good humidity. Use damaged potatoes first and never store green ones for eating.

Can you grow potatoes in containers or grow bags?

Yes. Use a container that holds at least 10–15 gallons for 2–3 seed pieces, with drainage holes. Start with 4–6 inches of potting mix, plant the seed pieces, cover with 3–4 inches of mix, and keep adding mix as the plants grow, the same way you would hill in the ground. Containers dry out fast, so check the moisture daily in warm weather. To harvest, tip the container out onto a tarp.

Frequently asked questions

How deep should you plant potatoes?

Plant seed pieces about 4 inches deep in a trench, or 3–4 inches deep in a container, then add more soil around the stems as the plants grow.

How long do potatoes take to grow?

Early varieties are ready in about 70–90 days and late varieties in about 100–130 days. You can dig a few new potatoes earlier, around 2–3 weeks after the plants flower.

Can you plant potatoes from the grocery store?

They may sprout, but many are treated to prevent sprouting and can carry disease. Certified seed potatoes are more reliable and give bigger harvests.

Why are my potatoes green?

Tubers turned green because they were exposed to light. Green parts contain solanine, which is bitter and can upset your stomach, so cut them away generously or discard very green potatoes. Hill soil or straw over the stems to prevent it.

How many potatoes do you get from one plant?

A healthy plant typically gives around 5–10 potatoes, depending on the variety, spacing, water and season length.

Should you water potatoes every day?

No. Water deeply once or twice a week to give about 1–2 inches in total, and check that the soil is moist a few inches down. Daily light watering keeps the surface wet and encourages disease.

The bottom line

Plant certified seed potatoes 2–4 weeks before your last frost, 4 inches deep and 10–12 inches apart, in full sun and loose, slightly acidic soil. Hill the stems twice, water evenly through flowering, and harvest new potatoes after the plants bloom or storage potatoes after the vines die back. Start by finding your last frost date by ZIP code.
