Before You Plant: Hardneck vs. Softneck & Variety Selection

Garlic falls into two main categories, and choosing between them is the first critical step.

  • Hardneck Garlic (Allium sativum var. ophioscorodon):
    • Characteristics: Produces a central woody stem (scape) that curls. Has fewer, larger cloves per bulb, arranged around the stem. Generally more cold-hardy.
    • Flavor: Often boasts a more complex, spicier flavor.
    • Best For: Colder climates (Zones 3-6). Excellent for roasting and fresh culinary use.
    • Popular Varieties: 'Music', 'German Red', 'Chesnok Red'.
  • Softneck Garlic (Allium sativum var. sativum):
    • Characteristics: Does not produce a scape. Has many smaller cloves, often in multiple layers. Braids well.
    • Flavor: Milder, more traditional garlic flavor.
    • Best For: Warmer climates (Zones 7-10). Better storage potential.
    • Popular Varieties: 'Inchelium Red', 'California Early', 'Silverskin'.

Expert Tip: For most home gardeners in temperate zones, hardneck varieties are often preferred for their ease of peeling, larger cloves, and delicious scapes. If you're unsure, try planting a few of each type to see what thrives best in your garden.

The Step-by-Step Guide to Growing Garlic

Garlic is a cool-season crop, best planted in the fall for a summer harvest in most climates. Follow these eight steps for a robust and flavorful crop.

Step 1: Preparing Your Prime Planting Site

Garlic thrives in full sun (at least 6-8 hours daily) and well-drained, fertile soil. Before planting, incorporate a generous 2-3 inch layer of organic compost or aged manure into the top 8-10 inches of your bed. This builds the rich, loose soil structure garlic craves.

Garlic prefers a slightly acidic to neutral pH, between 6.0 and 7.0. If your soil is heavy clay, consider planting in raised beds or amending heavily with sand and organic matter to improve drainage. For more detailed insights, refer to our guide on building healthy garden soil.

Step 2: Breaking the Bulb & Selecting Cloves



Just before planting, gently separate your garlic bulbs into individual cloves. Do this carefully to avoid damaging the basal plate (the flat end where roots emerge). Select only the largest, healthiest cloves for planting; these will produce the largest bulbs.

Resist the temptation to plant the tiny inner cloves, as they will only yield small, unproductive bulbs.

Step 3: Precise Planting Depth and Spacing



Planting depth is crucial. Plant each clove with the pointed end facing up and the basal plate (root end) facing down, approximately 4-6 inches deep.

Space the cloves 6 inches apart within rows, and allow 12-18 inches between rows. This generous spacing provides ample room for bulb development and good air circulation, which helps prevent fungal issues.

Step 4: Winter Protection with a Layer of Mulch

Once planted, immediately cover your garlic bed with a 4-6 inch layer of organic mulch, such as straw or shredded leaves. This insulates the soil, protects the cloves from harsh winter freezes and thaws, and conserves moisture.



In spring, as the temperatures warm, you can pull back some of the mulch to allow the soil to heat up, or simply let the garlic shoots emerge through it.

Step 5: Consistent Watering & Fertilizing in Spring

As spring arrives and growth begins in earnest, consistent moisture is vital for bulb development. Aim for 1 inch of water per week, either from rainfall or irrigation. Reduce watering as the bulbs begin to mature in late spring/early summer.

Garlic is a moderate to heavy feeder. In early spring, when green shoots are about 6 inches tall, top-dress with a balanced organic fertilizer or a side-dressing of rich compost. Avoid high-nitrogen fertilizers late in the season, as this can prioritize leaf growth over bulb formation.

Step 6: Cutting Garlic Scapes (Hardneck Only)

If you're growing hardneck varieties, you'll notice a curling flower stalk, called a "scape," emerge in late spring. These are edible and delicious. More importantly, removing them directs the plant's energy into bulb development rather than seed production.



Cut the scape when it forms one or two curls, just above the top leaf. Enjoy them in stir-fries, pesto, or grilled.

Step 7: Recognizing the Perfect Harvest Time

Knowing exactly when to harvest is crucial for bulb size and storage potential. Resist the urge to pull too early. In most regions, garlic is ready to harvest in late June or July.



The key visual cue: when the bottom two to three leaves have turned yellow and started to brown, but the upper leaves are still predominantly green. If you wait until all the leaves are brown, the wrappers around the bulb may have deteriorated, leading to shorter storage life.

[Expert Tip]: Once you see the bottom leaves yellowing, stop watering entirely. This helps the bulbs begin to "cure" in the ground and prevents fungal issues.

Step 8: The Critical Curing Process for Storage

Immediately after harvesting, the bulbs need to be cured. This is not optional if you want your garlic to last for months. Gently loosen the soil around the bulbs and carefully lift them, leaving the stems and roots intact.



Bundle 5-10 bulbs together and hang them in a cool, dark, and well-ventilated location (a shed, garage, or even under a porch overhang works). Ensure good air circulation around each bundle. Cure for 3-4 weeks, or until the wrappers are papery dry and the neck is tight and stiff.

Storing Your Homegrown Garlic

Once fully cured, use a pair of shears to trim the roots to about 1/2 inch and cut the stems back to 1-2 inches above the bulb. For softneck varieties, this is when you can consider turning them into beautiful and practical garlic braids.

Store your cured garlic in mesh bags, open baskets, or tied braids in a cool (10-15°C / 50-60°F), dark, and dry place with good air circulation. Do not store in the refrigerator, as this can trigger sprouting.

Troubleshooting Common Garlic Growing Problems

Symptom Likely Cause Solution
Small, underdeveloped bulbs Too little sun; insufficient nutrients; premature harvest; cloves too small at planting; poor spacing. Ensure full sun, fertile soil, consistent feeding in spring, proper clove selection and spacing. Harvest at correct time.
Leaves yellowing prematurely (before harvest time) Nitrogen deficiency; inconsistent watering; fungal disease (e.g., rust). Fertilize in spring with balanced organic fertilizer. Ensure consistent moisture. Check for orange/brown pustules (rust) and remove affected leaves.
Bulbs rotting in the ground Overwatering; poor drainage; fungal disease (e.g., white rot). Improve soil drainage with organic matter. Reduce watering as harvest approaches. Remove and destroy affected plants; do not replant garlic in affected soil for several years.
Garlic scapes don't form (hardneck varieties) Lack of sufficient cold dormancy (vernalization). Ensure you plant early enough in fall to allow for sufficient chilling hours before winter. Protect with mulch.

Frequently Asked Questions About Growing Garlic

1. When is the best time to plant garlic?

The best time to plant garlic in most temperate climates is in the fall, typically 4-6 weeks before the ground freezes solid. This allows the cloves to establish roots before winter dormancy and ensures a larger, more flavorful harvest the following summer.

2. Do you plant the whole garlic head?

No, you do not plant the whole garlic head. Instead, you gently break the head into individual cloves and plant each large, healthy clove separately. Each planted clove will grow into a new, full head of garlic.

3. How long does it take for garlic to grow?

Garlic has a long growing season. Planted in the fall, it typically takes 8-9 months to fully mature, with harvest usually occurring in late June or July of the following year. Spring-planted garlic (possible in warmer climates) will mature faster but generally produces smaller bulbs.

4. What kind of soil does garlic need?

Garlic thrives in rich, well-drained, loose soil with a slightly acidic to neutral pH (6.0-7.0). Heavy clay soils should be amended generously with organic matter like compost to improve drainage and prevent bulb rot. Avoid compacted soil at all costs.

5. How do you know when garlic is ready to harvest?

Garlic is ready to harvest when the bottom two to three leaves have turned yellow and started to brown, but the upper leaves are still green. This indicates the bulb is mature, but the protective wrappers are still intact. Stop watering about two weeks before this stage.
