Growing your own cabbage is one of gardening's most rewarding projects. Forget the bland, uniform heads from the supermarket; a homegrown cabbage offers superior flavor, incredible crispness, and deep satisfaction. Whether you dream of crunchy coleslaw, tangy sauerkraut, or hearty winter stews, this guide provides the definitive, step-by-step process to ensure a successful harvest.

Before You Plant: Choosing the Right Cabbage Variety

Your success begins with selecting a variety suited to your climate and goals. Cabbages are broadly categorized by their harvest time, which is crucial for planning.

  • Early-Season Varieties (60-80 days): These are smaller and perfect for a quick spring crop before the summer heat intensifies. Look for 'Golden Acre' or 'Early Jersey Wakefield'.
  • Mid-Season Varieties (80-100 days): Excellent all-rounders for a main summer harvest. 'Copenhagen Market' is a classic, reliable choice.
  • Late-Season Varieties (100+ days): These produce large, dense heads that are ideal for storage and making sauerkraut. 'Danish Ballhead' is a top performer.
  • Specialty Varieties: Consider 'Savoy' types with their crinkled, tender leaves, or vibrant 'Red Acre' for stunning color in salads.

For beginners, an early-season variety is often the most forgiving and provides a quick, confidence-boosting win.

Gathering your supplies beforehand makes the entire process smooth and efficient. You won't need a massive workshop, just a few quality basics. Your organized toolkit, as shown in the image, should be your starting point.

The Step-by-Step Guide to Growing Cabbage

Follow these seven steps methodically. Each one builds on the last, setting your plants up for vigorous growth and a productive harvest.

Step 1: Perfect Your Soil Preparation

Cabbage demands two things from its soil: fertility and good drainage. Before planting, work a generous 2-3 inch layer of compost or well-rotted manure into the top 8 inches of your garden bed. This fuels the massive leaf growth required to form a dense head.

Cabbages prefer a slightly acidic to neutral soil pH between 6.5 and 7.0. If you've had issues with brassicas before, a simple soil test is the most reliable way to know your starting point. Amend with lime to raise pH or sulfur to lower it, following the test kit's instructions.

Step 2: Sowing Seeds & Starting Indoors

For best results, start seeds indoors 6-8 weeks before your last expected spring frost. This gives you a strong, healthy transplant ready to thrive in the cool spring weather.



  1. Fill your seed trays with a sterile, damp seed-starting mix.
  2. Plant one seed per cell, about ¼ inch deep.
  3. Cover lightly with soil and gently mist with water.
  4. Place in a warm spot or on a heat mat. Germination should occur in 5-10 days.
  5. Once seedlings emerge, move them under grow lights or to a very sunny windowsill.

Step 3: The Critical "Hardening Off" Process

You cannot move seedlings directly from the controlled indoor environment to the harsh outdoors. They will suffer from shock. You must acclimate them over 7-10 days. Think of it as training your plants for the real world.



  • Day 1-2: Place seedlings in a sheltered, shady spot outdoors for 1-2 hours.
  • Day 3-4: Increase their time outside to 3-4 hours, introducing them to some gentle morning sun.
  • Day 5-7: Extend their outdoor time and gradually expose them to more direct sunlight and wind.
  • Day 8-10: Leave them out overnight, as long as temperatures remain above 4°C (40°F). They are now ready for their permanent home.

Step 4: Transplanting Seedlings into the Garden



Choose a cool, overcast day for transplanting to minimize stress on the plants. Space your hardened-off seedlings 18-24 inches apart. This seems like a lot of space now, but they will need it as they mature.

Dig a hole slightly larger than the seedling's root ball. Gently place the plant in the hole, ensuring it sits at the same depth it did in its pot. Firm the soil around the base and water it in thoroughly to settle the roots.

Step 5: Consistent Watering and Mulching

Cabbage heads are mostly water, so consistent moisture is non-negotiable for good head development. Aim for 1 to 1.5 inches of water per week, either from rain or your hose. Irregular watering can lead to stunted growth or split heads.

Once your transplants are established, apply a 2-inch layer of organic mulch around the base of each plant. This is a game-changer: it conserves soil moisture, keeps the roots cool, and suppresses competing weeds.

Step 6: Fertilizing for Healthy Head Formation

About three weeks after transplanting, it's time for a nutrient boost. Cabbage is a heavy feeder, especially of nitrogen, which fuels leaf growth. Side-dress your plants with a balanced organic fertilizer or a top-dressing of rich compost.

Think of this feeding as the fuel your cabbage plant needs to build its dense, heavy head. A second application can be beneficial about a month later, especially for late-season varieties.

Step 7: Protecting Your Crop from Pests



Nothing is more frustrating than seeing your beautiful plants destroyed by pests. The number one enemy is the Cabbage Worm (the larvae of the white Cabbage Butterfly). The moment you plant, you must protect them.

The single most effective, organic solution is to install a floating row cover immediately after transplanting. This physical barrier prevents the butterfly from ever laying its eggs on your leaves. It is a proactive strategy that works every time. A great first line of defense is also strategic companion planting.

[Safety First Warning]: If you choose to use pesticides, even organic ones like Bt (Bacillus thuringiensis), always apply them in the late evening to protect beneficial pollinators. Read and follow all label instructions precisely for application rates and personal protective equipment. Never spray on a windy day.

Harvesting Your Cabbage: The Final Reward

Knowing when to harvest is key. Most varieties will be ready 60-100 days after transplanting. Don't rely just on the calendar; use your hands. The head should feel solid, dense, and heavy for its size. Give it a firm squeeze—if it feels loose or soft, give it another week.



To harvest, use a sharp knife and cut the head off at the base of the plant, just above the lowest set of outer leaves. Leave the base and roots in the ground. On some varieties, this can encourage a secondary crop of small, sprout-like heads to grow from the stalk.

Storing Your Harvest for Lasting Freshness

Store your harvested cabbage unwashed in the crisper drawer of your refrigerator. Kept this way, it can last for several weeks, if not months for the dense storage varieties. Avoid washing it until you are ready to use it, as excess moisture encourages rot.

Holes chewed in leaves; green caterpillars present Cabbage Worms Use floating row covers from day one. Handpick worms. Apply Bt (Bacillus thuringiensis) as a last resort.
Head cracks or splits open Inconsistent watering (heavy rain after a dry spell) Ensure consistent moisture with a soaker hose and mulch. Harvest heads promptly when they are firm.

Frequently Asked Questions About Growing Cabbage

1. How long does it really take to grow cabbage?

It varies by variety. Early-season types can be ready in as little as 60 days after transplanting, while large, late-season storage varieties can take over 100 days. Always check the seed packet for the specific "days to maturity" for the variety you've chosen.

2. Does cabbage need full sun or can it grow in shade?

Cabbage needs full sun to thrive, which means at least 6-8 hours of direct sunlight per day. Without enough sun, the plants may grow leafy but will fail to form a dense, solid head.

3. Can I grow cabbage in a pot or container?

Absolutely! Choose a smaller, early-season variety. You'll need a large container, at least 5 gallons (18 liters) in size with excellent drainage holes. Container-grown plants dry out faster, so you'll need to be extra vigilant with watering.

4. My outer cabbage leaves are turning yellow. What's wrong?

Yellowing on the lowest, oldest leaves can be a sign of a nitrogen deficiency. It's the plant's way of saying it needs more fuel. Try side-dressing with a nitrogen-rich organic fertilizer or compost tea. If the yellowing is widespread or accompanied by wilting, investigate for root diseases like Clubroot.

5. What should I do after I've harvested the main head?

After cutting the main head, leave the plant's base and roots in the ground. With continued watering, many varieties will produce several small, secondary heads (like large Brussels sprouts) from the stalk. These "bonus" cabbages are a tender and delicious extra harvest. Once these are picked, you can remove the entire plant and add it to your compost pile.
