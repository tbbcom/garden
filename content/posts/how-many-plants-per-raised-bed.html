Quick answer

A 4×8 raised bed (32 sq ft) holds about 8 tomatoes, 10 peppers, 32 cucumbers or kale plants, or 288 bush beans if you fill the whole bed with one crop. Most gardeners mix crops instead. To find your own number, divide each bed side in inches by the plant spacing, drop the decimals and multiply the two results.

The most common raised bed in US backyards is 4 feet wide and 8 feet long, and the most common question is what to put in it. Plant too many and you get small harvests and disease; plant too few and you waste soil you paid for. The right number depends on the crop's mature size, not on how big the seedlings look in the pot.

This guide gives plant counts for a full 4 × 8 bed, quick numbers for other bed sizes and three mixed-bed plans that add up. To work out any other crop or bed shape, the plant spacing calculator does the arithmetic for you.

Key takeaways
  • Formula: (bed width in inches ÷ spacing) × (bed length in inches ÷ spacing), rounded down on each side.
  • Full 4 × 8 bed, one crop: 8 tomatoes at 24 in, 10 peppers at 18 in, 32 kale at 12 in, 128 garlic at 6 in.
  • Large crops first: plan tomatoes, peppers and squash, then fill gaps with fast greens and roots.
  • Vertical crops save space: a trellised cucumber or pole bean row uses a strip 6 inches wide.
  • Leave edge room: keep the first row 6 to 12 inches from the board so mature plants don't flop over the edge.

How many plants fit in a 4×8 raised bed?

A 4 × 8 bed measures 48 in × 96 in. With plants set on a square grid at a given spacing, the maximum number is the width divided by the spacing times the length divided by the spacing, each rounded down. These counts treat the whole bed as plantable. In practice, trim a few plants off the large-crop numbers to leave a 3 to 6 inch buffer at the edge. Tomatoes at 24 in spacing, for example, fit in two rows of four, but a 4-foot-wide bed gives each row only a 12-inch margin, which is plenty.

What does a full 4×8 mixed-bed plan look like?

Few gardeners want 512 carrots. A mixed bed mixes tall and small crops, early and late ones, and usually yields something fresh every week. Here are three plans for a 4 × 8 bed, with plant counts that fit the spacing in the chart above.

Plan 1: Salsa bed

  • 2 tomatoes at 24 in (north end, staked)
  • 4 peppers at 18 in (middle)
  • 2 jalapeño or hot pepper plants (south of the peppers)
  • 1 square foot of cilantro, sown every 3 weeks
  • Onions at 4 in along the south edge: 12 plants

Plan 2: Salad and snack bed

  • One 4-ft trellis of cucumbers, 4 plants at 12 in
  • 16 leaf lettuce plants at 6 in
  • 32 carrots at 3 in
  • 16 radishes at 3 in, re-sown every 2 weeks
  • 6 basil plants at 12 in

Plan 3: Cool-season greens bed

  • 8 broccoli or cabbage at 18 in
  • 6 kale or collards at 12 in
  • 20 spinach at 4 in
  • 16 beets at 4 in

Should you plant the same number of plants every year?

Not always. Three factors can push you above or below the chart numbers:

  • Variety size. Determinate (bush) tomatoes can go as close as 18 inches; big indeterminate types do better at 24 to 36 inches. Check the tag.
  • Climate. In hot, humid areas, wider spacing improves airflow and cuts fungal disease. In dry, cool climates, slightly tighter spacing is fine.
  • Soil and feeding. Dense planting needs rich soil. If your bed is only 6 inches deep or you don't feed regularly, plant at the wider end of the range.

How do you avoid overcrowding a raised bed?

  1. Draw the mature size, not the seedling. A 6-inch tomato transplant becomes a 3-foot plant. Sketch circles at mature width on paper before you plant.
  2. Plant tall crops on the north side so they don't cast shade on shorter ones.
  3. Grow up where you can. Cucumbers, pole beans, peas and small squash on a trellis use a fraction of the floor space.
  4. Fill gaps with fast crops. Radishes and baby lettuce finish in 3 to 6 weeks, before the big plants need the room.
  5. Thin early. Crowded seedlings produce fewer and smaller vegetables, and root crops can fail entirely.
Don't pack tomatoes shoulder to shoulder. Crowded tomato plants stay wet after rain or watering, which favors early blight, septoria leaf spot and other fungal diseases. If your bed is small, grow fewer plants and prune lower leaves to improve airflow.

How much soil does a 4×8 raised bed need for all those plants?

A 4 × 8 bed filled 12 inches deep holds 32 cubic feet, or about 1.19 cubic yards of soil. At 8 inches deep it is 21.3 cubic feet (0.79 cubic yards). Plants at the dense end of the range rely on that soil for water and nutrients, so don't skimp. The soil calculator gives exact volumes for your depth and bag size.

Frequently asked questions

How many tomato plants can I put in a 4x8 raised bed?

Eight staked tomatoes fit at 24 inch spacing, but 4 to 6 plants give better airflow and are easier to prune and pick. Two rows of three or four plants, running the length of the bed, work well.

How many pepper plants can I grow in a 4x8 bed?

Up to 10 pepper plants fit at 18 inch spacing. Most people plant 6 to 8 and use the remaining space for herbs or onions.

Can I plant different vegetables right next to each other in a raised bed?

Yes. Mixing crops is normal in a raised bed. Just keep each plant at its own spacing and put tall crops on the north side so they don't shade short ones.

How far from the edge should I plant?

Leave 3 to 6 inches for small crops and 6 to 12 inches for large ones like tomatoes and squash. That keeps stems and leaves from draping over the board.

How many plants fit in a 4x4 raised bed?

A 4 × 4 bed holds 4 tomatoes or peppers at 24 inch spacing, 16 kale plants at 12 inches, or 64 lettuce at 6 inches.

Does a bigger raised bed mean more yield?

Only if you fertilize and water enough. A bigger bed gives more room, but yield depends on sun, soil and spacing, not bed size alone.

The bottom line

A 4 × 8 raised bed holds 8 staked tomatoes, 10 peppers, 32 kale plants or hundreds of small roots, but you'll get better harvests from a mixed plan that gives each crop its full spacing.
