Quick answer

Spring garden work follows soil temperature and your last frost date, not the calendar. In early spring, clean up, test soil and sow peas and spinach as soon as soil can be worked. Plant tomatoes and peppers only after the last frost, when nights stay above 50°F, and start crabgrass prevention when soil reaches about 55°F.

Spring arrives at different times across the US, so a checklist tied to dates only works for one region. This one is tied to triggers you can measure: the soil is workable, the last frost has passed, the soil is warm enough for a given crop.

Key takeaways
  • Soil workable: squeeze a handful; if it crumbles instead of staying in a wet ball, you can dig.
  • Cool-season crops (peas, spinach, lettuce, radish) go in 4–6 weeks before the last frost, when soil is roughly 40°F or above.
  • Warm-season crops (tomato, pepper, cucumber, squash) wait until after the last frost and soil near 60°F or warmer.
  • Crabgrass preventer goes down when soil temperature reaches about 55°F for several days.
  • Mulch after the soil warms, 2–3 inches deep.

What should you do first in early spring (February to April)?

Early spring is cleanup and prep. You are waiting for soil to thaw and dry, so focus on jobs that don't need warm ground.

  1. Clear winter debris from beds, but wait for steady 50°F days before removing leaf litter and stems where beneficial insects may still be sheltering.
  2. Cut back ornamental grasses and last year's perennial stems before new growth appears.
  3. Test your soil (pH and nutrients) before you fertilize or lime. See our guide to calculating soil and amendment volumes.
  4. Prune summer-flowering shrubs and roses; leave lilac and forsythia until they finish blooming.
  5. Sharpen and clean tools, and check hoses and irrigation for winter damage.
  6. Start seeds indoors for tomatoes, peppers and other warm-season crops about 6–8 weeks before the last frost.

When is the soil ready to work?

Digging wet soil in spring creates hard clods that last all season. Use the squeeze test: grab a handful from 4 inches down and squeeze. If it forms a ball that holds together when you poke it, it's too wet. If it crumbles, go ahead. A soil thermometer pushed 2 inches deep in the morning tells you which crops can go in.

What should you do in mid spring (March to May)?

Mid spring is the main planting window for most of the country, usually from a few weeks before to about the last frost.

  • Add compost to beds: 1–2 inches, worked into the top few inches or spread on the surface. A 4×8 bed takes 2.7–5.3 cubic feet.
  • Direct-sow cool-season crops and succession-plant lettuce and radish every two weeks.
  • Harden off seedlings over 7–10 days by putting them outdoors for a few hours more each day.
  • Divide perennials such as hostas and daylilies as shoots emerge.
  • Feed the lawn if your grass is cool-season and the soil is above about 55°F; warm-season lawns wait until they have fully greened up.
  • Apply pre-emergent crabgrass control when soil is about 55°F for several days (forsythia bloom is a traditional signal); skip it where you plan to overseed.

What should you do in late spring (April to June)?

After the last frost date, switch to warm-season crops and mulch.

  1. Transplant tomatoes, peppers and eggplant once nights stay above 50°F.
  2. Sow beans, corn, cucumbers, squash and melons when soil reaches 60–70°F depending on the crop.
  3. Mulch with 2–3 inches of straw, shredded leaves or bark once the soil has warmed. Mulching cold soil keeps it cold.
  4. Set up trellises and cages before plants need them, so you don't damage roots later.
  5. Water deeply but less often, about 1 inch of water per week including rain for most vegetable gardens.
  6. Watch for pests such as aphids and cutworms; protect transplants with collars of cardboard or foil around stems.

Don't plant warm-season crops just because the days feel warm. A single cold night after the last average frost date can still kill tomatoes. Check the 10-day forecast and keep frost cloth ready until nights are reliably above 40–45°F.

Spring lawn and tree checklist

  • Rake matted leaves and dead grass gently; save heavy dethatching for later.
  • Overseed bare patches of cool-season lawn in early spring if soil is above about 50°F, or wait until fall for best results.
  • Mow at the recommended height for your grass; avoid removing more than one-third of the blade at once.
  • Mulch trees in a wide ring, 2–3 inches deep and pulled back from the trunk.
  • Check for winter damage on shrubs and wait for new growth before cutting back dead-looking stems.

Spring gardening mistakes to avoid

  1. Planting by date, not conditions. A warm week in March doesn't change your frost risk.
  2. Working wet soil. It compacts and dries into clods.
  3. Skipping hardening off. Indoor seedlings can sunburn or wilt within hours.
  4. Over-fertilizing. Lush growth attracts pests; use a soil test.
  5. Buying everything at once. Plants for cool-season and warm-season crops need different dates.

How do you keep a spring garden plan simple?

Write your average last frost date at the top of a page, then list each crop with a sow or transplant date counted from it. Add the date you will harden off seedlings and the date you plan to mulch. Keeping the plan on one page makes it easy to adjust when a late cold snap or a warm spell changes the timing, and it gives you something to compare against next year.

Take notes as you go: which varieties did well, what you planted too early and how long the soil took to dry out. A season of notes is worth more than any generic calendar.

Frequently asked questions

What should I do in the garden first in spring?

Start with cleanup, a soil test and sharpening tools while the ground is still cold. Sow peas and spinach as soon as the soil is workable.

How do I know when it is safe to plant outside?

For cool-season crops, the soil must be workable and above about 40°F. For tomatoes and peppers, wait until after your average last frost and until nights stay above 50°F.

When should I put down crabgrass preventer?

Apply it when soil temperature at 2 inches deep reaches about 55°F for several consecutive days, typically before forsythia drops its blooms.

Should I fertilize my garden beds in spring?

Add compost every spring and use a soil test to guide any extra fertilizer. Heavy-feeding crops like tomatoes and squash benefit from a balanced fertilizer at planting.

How often should I water new spring plantings?

Keep seeds evenly moist until they sprout; for transplants, water deeply once or twice a week so roots grow down, adjusting for rain.

The bottom line

Match each job to a trigger: workable soil, the last frost, or a soil temperature. Clean up and test early, sow cool-season crops first, then plant warm-season crops after frost and mulch once the ground has warmed.
