Quick answer

A raised bed should hold at least 6 to 8 inches of soil for greens and herbs and 12 inches for most vegetables, and 18 inches is better for tomatoes, carrots and potatoes. If the bed sits on loosened native soil, roots can grow deeper than the frame. On concrete or hard clay, build the full depth you need.

Depth is the decision that most affects both your harvest and your budget. A taller bed holds more soil, which costs more, but it also drains better, reduces bending and gives roots room. A shallow bed is cheap and quick to fill, but it dries faster and limits some crops.

This guide shows how deep to build for each vegetable, how much soil each depth needs, and when a deeper bed is worth it. The soil calculator converts any depth into cubic yards and bags.

Key takeaways
  • Minimum depth: 6 in for lettuce, radishes and herbs; 12 in for most vegetables.
  • Best depth for tomatoes, carrots, potatoes and squash: 12–18 in or more.
  • Native soil counts: on loosened ground, roots go below the frame; on concrete or compacted fill, they can't.
  • Soil volume doubles when depth doubles. A 4×8 bed needs 16 cu ft at 6 in and 32 cu ft at 12 in.
  • Height and soil depth differ: a bed can be built taller than the soil it holds.

How deep does a raised bed need to be for vegetables?

Most vegetable roots do their feeding in the top 6 to 12 inches of soil, so a bed with 12 inches of good soil grows nearly every common crop. Deeper soil helps in hot or dry weather because it holds more water and keeps roots cooler. The minimums below assume the bed sits on ground that has been loosened, so roots can also reach beyond the frame.

Does it matter what is under the bed?

Yes. This is the factor that changes the answer the most.

On loosened native soil

If the bed sits on lawn or garden soil and you remove the grass (or smother it with cardboard), roots of tomatoes, peppers and other crops will grow into the ground below. A 6- to 12-inch bed can then perform like a much deeper one. Loosen the top few inches with a fork before placing the frame for best results.

On concrete, pavers or compacted subsoil

If the bottom is hard, roots stop at the bottom of your soil. Build at least 12 inches deep, and 18 or more for tomatoes, squash and root crops. A bed on a patio also dries out faster and gets hot, so deeper soil is more helpful there. Make sure it can drain through holes or gaps at the base.

Over landscape fabric or a weed barrier

Plastic or woven landscape fabric stops roots and slows drainage. Use plain cardboard, which breaks down within a season, or no barrier at all, if you want roots to reach the ground.

Is a deeper raised bed always better?

Not always. Deeper beds drain better, stay cooler and hold more water, but they cost more soil and lumber, and the extra depth at the bottom is rarely used by shallow roots.

  • Worth it: for tomatoes and root crops, on concrete, for gardeners who want a bed at waist height, or in hot, dry regions.
  • Not needed: for lettuce and herbs, or on good native soil.
  • Accessibility: a 24- to 30-inch bed can be reached from a chair or without bending. A bed this tall needs sturdy construction and a lot of soil; see the volumes above.

How can you fill a deep raised bed without buying so much soil?

A 24-inch bed needs 64 cubic feet in a 4 × 8 footprint, which is the most expensive part of the project. Some ways to reduce cost without hurting plants:

  1. Put coarse organic material in the bottom. Logs, branches and leaves (the idea behind hugelkultur) fill space and slowly decompose. Use well-seasoned wood, because fresh wood ties up nitrogen as it rots.
  2. Use native soil. If your yard soil is decent, dig and mix 1 part into 2 parts compost-rich mix for the lower layer.
  3. Buy bulk. Bagged soil costs more per cubic foot than bulk soil delivered by the yard.
  4. Put the best mix on top. Fill the top 8 to 12 inches with quality raised bed mix where roots are active.

Whatever you use, expect the fill to settle by 15 to 25 percent over a season, and top up with 1 to 2 inches of compost each spring.

Don't fill with fresh wood chips or sawdust alone. Fresh wood pulls nitrogen from the soil as it decomposes and starves plants. Keep wood in the lower layers, well below the root zone, or use it only as mulch on top.

What mistakes do gardeners make with raised bed depth?

  1. Building 6 inches on concrete and expecting tomatoes to thrive.
  2. Mixing up board height and soil depth. A 12-inch board filled to 8 inches holds only 8 inches of soil.
  3. Using fabric under the bed that prevents roots and water from moving down.
  4. Not allowing for settling. New fill sinks by 15 to 25 percent. Fill to the top and refill next spring.
  5. Skipping drainage on a hard surface. Without it, roots sit in water and rot.

Frequently asked questions

Is a 6-inch deep raised bed enough?

It is enough for lettuce, spinach, radishes, herbs and green onions, especially over loosened ground. Tomatoes, carrots and potatoes will do better with 12 inches or more.

Is 12 inches deep enough for tomatoes?

Yes, if the bed sits on loosened native soil so roots can go deeper. In a bed on concrete, aim for 18 inches or more to avoid drying and stress.

How tall should a raised bed be for easy access?

For an easy reach, 24 to 30 inches puts the soil at a comfortable height without bending. For wheelchair access, 24 to 30 inches with a narrow width works well.

Do I need to dig out the ground under a raised bed?

No. Just remove or smother the grass and loosen the top few inches of soil so roots and water can move down. Digging out a deep hole is rarely worth it.

How deep should the soil be for carrots in a raised bed?

For long varieties, plan on 12 inches of loose, stone-free soil. For shorter types like Chantenay or round Parisian, 8 to 10 inches is enough.

Can a raised bed be too deep?

It can't harm plants, but it can waste soil and money. Beyond 18 to 24 inches, most vegetable roots don't use the extra depth unless the bed is on concrete.

The bottom line

Build a raised bed 6 to 8 inches deep for greens and herbs, 12 inches for most vegetables and 18 inches or more for tomatoes, carrots and potatoes, and go deeper if it sits on concrete or compacted soil. A 4 × 8 bed needs 32 cubic feet at 12 inches and 48 cubic feet at 18. Work out the exact amount with the soil calculator before you order.
