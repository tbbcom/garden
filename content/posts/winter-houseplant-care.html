Quick answer

In winter, give houseplants more light, less water and no fertilizer. Move plants to the brightest window, water only when the top 1–2 inches of soil are dry, keep them away from heaters and cold glass, and aim for 40–50% humidity and 65–75°F. Growth slows in low light, so most plants need far less care.

Houseplants are tropical or subtropical plants kept in rooms that are dry, drafty and dim in winter. The usual winter problems, such as yellow leaves, leaf drop, crispy brown tips and mushy stems, almost always come from three things: too little light, too much water and dry air.

This guide fixes each one in turn, with simple checks rather than fixed schedules. If you keep plants outdoors in summer, our planting calendar helps with bringing them in before frost.

Key takeaways
  • Light: winter sun is weaker and shorter; move plants closer to a south or west window.
  • Water: check the soil first; most plants need water less often in winter.
  • Humidity: heated homes often drop below 30%; aim for 40–50% for tropical plants.
  • Temperature: 65–75°F days and no colder than about 55–60°F nights for most tropicals.
  • Feeding: pause or cut back fertilizer from late fall to late winter.

Why do houseplants struggle in winter?

Three changes hit at once. Day length shortens to nine or ten hours in the northern US, and the sun sits lower, so light through a window can be much weaker than in summer. Indoor heating dries the air, often to 20–30% relative humidity. And plants grow slowly, which means they use less water and nutrients, but many people keep watering and feeding on the summer schedule.

How much light do houseplants need in winter?

Light drops sharply with distance from the glass. A plant 3 feet from a window can receive far less light than one on the sill. Move plants closer in winter, and rotate pots a quarter turn weekly so growth stays even.

If a room is too dark, add a full-spectrum LED grow light 6–12 inches above the plants for roughly 10–14 hours a day on a timer. Low-light tolerant plants such as snake plant and ZZ plant survive on very little; sun lovers do not.

Wipe dust off leaves with a damp cloth every month or two. A film of dust blocks light that is already scarce.

How often should you water houseplants in winter?

Water when the plant needs it, not on a calendar. Slow growth and cooler rooms mean soil stays wet longer, and overwatering is the leading cause of death for houseplants.

The finger test

Push a finger 1–2 inches into the potting mix. For most foliage plants, water when it feels dry at that depth. For succulents and cacti, let the whole pot dry out. For ferns and peace lilies, water when the top inch is dry.

How to water

  1. Use room-temperature water; cold water from a tap can shock roots.
  2. Water slowly until it runs out the drainage holes.
  3. Empty the saucer after 15–30 minutes so roots do not sit in water.
  4. Water in the morning so the pot dries during the day.
Yellow leaves and soft, dark stems usually mean overwatering, not underwatering. If the soil smells sour or the pot feels heavy days after watering, let it dry out, and repot into fresh mix if roots look brown and mushy.

How do you raise humidity for houseplants?

Most common houseplants, such as pothos and spider plants, tolerate average home humidity. Tropicals like calathea, ferns and orchids prefer 40–60%. Brown leaf tips and edges are a classic sign of dry air.

  • Group plants together. Transpiration raises humidity around them slightly.
  • Use a humidifier. It is the only method that raises room humidity measurably. A cheap hygrometer shows the level.
  • Pebble trays (a tray of wet stones under the pot, with the pot above the water line) help a little in the immediate area.
  • Skip misting. It raises humidity for minutes and can promote leaf spots.
  • Move plants out of bathrooms with no windows or kitchens with no light; humidity doesn't replace light.

What temperature do houseplants need in winter?

Most tropical houseplants do well at 65–75°F in the day and cooler at night, but not below about 55–60°F. Cold-sensitive plants such as African violets, orchids and calatheas suffer below 60°F. Cactus and some succulents tolerate cooler rooms of 50–55°F, and a cool winter rest helps Christmas cactus bloom.

  • Keep plants off cold windowsills at night. Glass can be many degrees colder than the room. Pull pots back or slide a sheet of cardboard between pot and glass.
  • Keep away from heat vents, radiators and fireplaces, which dry plants and scorch leaves.
  • Avoid drafts from exterior doors that open repeatedly in winter.

Should you fertilize or repot in winter?

Generally, no. Plants growing slowly in low light cannot use fertilizer, and salts build up in the soil and burn roots. Stop feeding from about late fall (October or November) and restart in spring when new growth appears, at the label rate. Plants under grow lights that are actively growing can be fed at half strength.

Repotting is also better in spring. Do it in winter only if the plant is root-bound and struggling, or if you must replace soggy mix.

How do you prevent pests in winter?

Dry indoor air favors spider mites; overwatering favors fungus gnats and mealybugs.

  • Inspect leaf undersides every couple of weeks for webbing, stippling or small white cottony spots.
  • Isolate infested plants and rinse them in the shower, then use insecticidal soap per the label.
  • For fungus gnats, let the top inch of soil dry and use yellow sticky traps.
  • Quarantine plants that spent summer outdoors for two weeks before putting them with others.

6 winter houseplant mistakes

  1. Watering on a schedule. Check the soil instead.
  2. Leaving plants behind curtains or on cold sills. Cold glass and dim light slow growth.
  3. Placing plants near heaters. They dry quickly and scorch.
  4. Feeding all winter. Salt build-up burns roots.
  5. Moving plants constantly. Frequent moves cause leaf drop.
  6. Misting instead of fixing humidity. The effect disappears in minutes.

Which houseplants are easiest in winter?

Some plants shrug off low light and dry air, which makes them good choices if you want fewer problems.

  • Snake plant and ZZ plant: tolerate dim rooms and very infrequent watering.
  • Pothos and philodendron: forgiving, and quick to recover from a missed watering.
  • Spider plant and cast iron plant: handle average indoor humidity and cooler rooms.
  • Jade and aloe: need bright light but very little water in winter.

The fussiest are calathea, ferns, fiddle-leaf fig and orchids, which dislike drafts, dry air and sudden changes. Give them the best spot you have or accept a few lost leaves until spring.

How do you move plants safely in and out of winter conditions?

If your plants spent summer outdoors, bring them inside before nights fall below about 50°F, rinse the leaves, check for pests and give them a week or two to adjust to lower light. They will likely drop some leaves as they adapt; this is normal. In spring, reverse the process gradually, moving plants to shade first and into brighter light over a week or two to prevent sunburn.

Frequently asked questions

Why are my houseplant leaves turning yellow in winter?

The most common causes are overwatering, low light and cold drafts. Check the soil moisture first, then move the plant to a brighter, warmer spot. A few lower leaves yellowing is normal as plants slow down.

Do houseplants need less water in winter?

Usually yes. Slower growth and cooler, darker rooms mean the soil takes longer to dry. Water only when the top 1–2 inches are dry.

Is it OK to put houseplants outside in winter?

No, not in most of the US. Tropical houseplants are damaged below about 50°F and killed by frost, so keep them indoors until all danger of frost has passed.

Do I need a grow light for houseplants in winter?

Not for low-light plants, but sun-loving species such as succulents, citrus and herbs benefit from an LED grow light on a timer when natural light is weak.

When should I start fertilizing houseplants again?

Restart in spring, around March or April, once you see new leaves and growth and days are longer. Use the label rate or half strength to begin.

The bottom line

Winter houseplant care is about doing less, in the right places: move plants to light, water by checking the soil, add humidity where plants need it and hold the fertilizer until spring. For potting-mix volumes when you repot, use the soil calculator.
