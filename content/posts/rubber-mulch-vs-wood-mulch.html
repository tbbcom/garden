Quick answer

Wood mulch is cheaper and feeds the soil; rubber mulch lasts many years but costs several times more and adds no nutrients. In the example below, 200 sq ft costs about $74 in wood mulch at 3 inches against about $438 in rubber. Wood is the better choice for plant beds; rubber suits playgrounds and low-maintenance borders away from the house.

Rubber mulch is sold as the permanent option: never fades, never washes away, never needs refreshing. Wood mulch is the traditional choice that rots away and has to be redone. Both claims have a catch, and the better pick depends on the spot.

Key takeaways
  • Upfront cost: rubber is often 4–6 times more per cubic yard than wood in the examples below.
  • Lifespan: rubber does not decompose; wood breaks down over one to three years and needs top-ups.
  • Soil: wood feeds soil as it rots; rubber does not.
  • Safety: rubber burns hot and is hard to extinguish, so keep it away from the house and fire sources.
  • Best uses: rubber for playgrounds and permanent borders; wood for gardens, trees and veggies.

What is the difference between rubber and wood mulch?

Wood mulch is shredded bark, chips or ground wood. It is an organic material: it holds moisture, keeps the soil cooler, then decomposes and improves the soil. Rubber mulch is shredded recycled tires, usually sold in nuggets or chips and often dyed brown, red or black. It is inorganic, so it does not rot, but it also adds nothing to the soil.

Which is cheaper, rubber or wood mulch?

Wood wins on upfront price by a wide margin. How long does each type last?

Wood mulch typically needs refreshing every one to two years in most climates, since shredded types break down faster than coarse chips or large bark. Rubber mulch is commonly marketed with a lifespan of ten years or more; since it does not decompose, it stays in place until you remove it. In practice, rubber can slowly thin from wind, foot traffic, mowing and washout, and the dye may fade.

Which is better for plants and soil?

For plant growth, wood wins. As it decomposes, it adds organic matter, feeds soil life and improves structure. Rubber holds heat, does not add nutrients and does nothing for soil structure. Because tires can contain compounds such as zinc, several university extension resources advise against using rubber mulch around vegetables and berries, and some caution against it for trees and shrubs where runoff or buildup could be a concern. Research results vary by plant, so this is a reason to be cautious rather than a verdict.

Weed control is similar when both are laid 2–3 inches deep over a clean bed. Rubber is slightly better at not blowing away; wood is better at being easily removed and replaced.

Is rubber mulch a fire risk?

Both can burn, and wildfire guidance warns that embers ignite any combustible mulch. Rubber is a particular concern because once it is burning it gives off heavy smoke, burns hot and is hard to put out. Fire-safety guidance generally recommends keeping any combustible mulch, rubber included, a few feet from the foundation, vents and wooden decks, and never next to grills, fire pits or places where cigarettes are discarded. Gravel or pavers are the best choice immediately beside a house in wildfire-prone areas.

Keep rubber mulch away from the house and from heat sources. It can reach high surface temperatures in full sun, so avoid it where bare feet or pets play, and think twice about hot, dry climates. Choose a non-combustible ring of gravel or pavers around foundations.

What about playgrounds?

Rubber is common on playgrounds because it cushions falls. The US Consumer Product Safety Commission playground handbook lists loose-fill depths by fall height; for a 10-foot fall height, it lists about 9 inches of wood chips and about 6 inches of shredded rubber, uncompressed. Those depths are far deeper than the 2–3 inches used in garden beds. Playground surfacing needs to meet impact-attenuation standards and be maintained, so for a play area use the CPSC handbook and ASTM F1292 as your guide, or consult a certified playground inspector.

Bonded or poured-in-place rubber surfacing is different from loose rubber mulch and is installed professionally.

How do you install rubber mulch if you choose it?

  1. Clear and weed the bed completely, because rubber is very hard to separate from soil later.
  2. Lay landscape fabric or a weed barrier if you want a clean removal later, and cut slits for plants.
  3. Spread 2–3 inches (about 1.2 to 1.9 cubic yards per 200 sq ft) and rake it level.
  4. Edge the bed with a firm border so it stays out of the lawn, where it can damage mower blades.

Wind-blown leaves and dust settle on top of any mulch, so rubber still needs the occasional raking and weeding.

Which should you choose?

  1. Vegetable gardens, perennial beds and trees: wood (or straw, leaves, compost).
  2. Playgrounds: follow safety standards; rubber and engineered wood fiber are both used at the proper depth.
  3. Low-maintenance borders away from the house: rubber is an option if you accept the cost and the lack of soil benefit.
  4. Next to the foundation: use gravel or pavers rather than either.
  5. Tight budget or big area: wood chips from a local tree service or landscape yard.

Frequently asked questions

Does rubber mulch attract bugs?

Rubber is not a food source so it does not draw termites or ants the way damp wood can. Insects can still shelter under any mulch, so keep it off the foundation.

Is rubber mulch bad for the soil?

It does not improve soil, and tires can contain zinc and other compounds that some extension sources advise against around edible plants. For soil health, organic mulch is better.

How much rubber mulch do I need?

The same volume as wood at the same depth: square feet × depth in inches ÷ 324 gives cubic yards. 200 sq ft at 3 inches needs 1.85 cubic yards.

Can you put rubber mulch over wood mulch?

You can, but it is better to remove the wood first. Wood decomposes underneath, leaves the layer uneven and can make the rubber sink and mix into soil.

Which mulch is better for a sloped yard?

Shredded wood mats together and stays put on slopes. Rubber also resists washing but is harder to rake back into place, so shredded wood is a good default.

Does rubber mulch get hot in summer?

Yes. Dark rubber can become noticeably hotter than wood in direct sun, so avoid it where children play barefoot and in very hot climates.

The bottom line

Choose wood mulch for gardens, trees and vegetable beds, where soil health matters and the lower cost wins. Consider rubber only for playgrounds or permanent borders away from the house, and budget several times more. Price the wood option in seconds with the mulch calculator.
