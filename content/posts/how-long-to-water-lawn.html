Quick answer

Most lawns need 1 to 1.5 inches of water per week, including rain. With rotor sprinklers (about 0.5 in/hr) that is 2–3 hours per week; with spray heads (about 1.5 in/hr) it is 40–60 minutes. Split it into two or three deep waterings, early in the morning, rather than a little every day.

How long to water your lawn is really two questions: how much water the grass needs, and how fast your sprinklers deliver it. Run time is just the first number divided by the second. Guess it, and you end up with either a soggy lawn full of fungus or brown patches by July.

This guide shows how to measure your own sprinkler output with a simple can test, gives run times for common sprinkler types and soils, and explains cycle-and-soak watering for slopes and clay. If you'd rather skip the arithmetic, the irrigation run time calculator does it from your numbers.

Key takeaways
  • Target: 1–1.5 in per week for cool-season grass, about 1 in for warm-season grass, rain included.
  • Formula: run time (minutes) = inches needed ÷ sprinkler output (in/hr) × 60.
  • Output varies: rotors apply about 0.4–0.6 in/hr, standard spray heads 1.5–2 in/hr.
  • Frequency: 2–3 deep waterings a week beat daily light watering.
  • Timing: between about 4 and 9 a.m., when wind and heat are lowest.

How much water does a lawn need per week?

The widely used guideline from university extension services is 1 to 1.5 inches per week during the growing season for cool-season grasses, and about 1 inch for warm-season grasses, which use less water. That total includes rainfall. A half-inch rain on Tuesday means you only need to supply the other half inch.

Hot, windy weather in July can push needs above 1.5 inches, especially in sandy soil. Cool spring and fall weeks may need only half an inch. The grass tells you: when it takes on a dull blue-gray tint, or footprints stay pressed into it, it is thirsty.

How do you measure how fast your sprinkler waters?

Do the "tuna can test." It takes about 30 minutes and turns guesswork into a real run time.

  1. Set 5–6 straight-sided, flat-bottomed cans (tuna or cat food cans work) at different spots in one watering zone.
  2. Run that zone for exactly 15 minutes.
  3. Measure the depth of water in each can with a ruler and add up the readings.
  4. Divide the total by the number of cans to get the average depth, then multiply by 4 to get inches per hour.

Example: six cans average 0.25 inch after 15 minutes. That is 0.25 × 4 = 1.0 in/hr. If the cans vary a lot (some at 0.1, some at 0.4), you have uneven coverage; look for clogged or misaligned heads.

How often should you water: daily or a few times a week?

Water deeply and infrequently. Frequent shallow watering keeps roots near the surface where heat and drought hit them first, and it keeps the leaf blades wet, which encourages disease. Deep watering, 0.5 to 0.75 inch per session, trains roots to reach down.

A practical pattern for most lawns is two sessions a week in cool weather and three in midsummer. Water new seed or sod differently: new seed needs light watering once or twice a day until it germinates.

How does soil type change your watering time?

Soil decides how much water the ground can take before it runs off. Sand drinks quickly but holds little; clay holds plenty but takes it in slowly.

Soil typeApprox. water intakeBest approach
Sandy1–2 in/hr or moreSmaller, more frequent sessions (about 0.5 in, 3 times a week)
Loam0.3–1 in/hrStandard: 0.5–0.75 in, 2 times a week
Clay0.1–0.3 in/hrCycle and soak (below), watch for runoff

If water pools or runs onto the sidewalk before your cycle is done, the soil can't absorb it that fast. That is when to use cycle and soak.

What is cycle and soak watering?

Instead of one long run, split the time into two or three short cycles with 30–60 minutes between them. A zone that needs 30 minutes might run 10 minutes, rest, then repeat twice. Most modern controllers have a setting for it. It is especially helpful on slopes and clay soil.

What is the best time of day to water a lawn?

Early morning, roughly 4 to 9 a.m., is best. Wind is low, the sun is not yet evaporating water, and the grass dries by midday, which cuts disease. Evening watering leaves grass wet through the night, which raises the risk of fungal problems. Midday watering works but wastes water to evaporation.

What watering mistakes should you avoid?

Watch for local rules. Many cities and water districts restrict lawn watering days or hours, especially in drought. Check yours before you set the timer.
  1. Setting every zone to the same time. Sun, shade, slopes and different head types mean zones need different minutes.
  2. Ignoring rain. A rain sensor or a skipped cycle after a good storm saves water and money.
  3. Watering daily for 10 minutes. It feels responsible but encourages shallow roots.
  4. Never checking heads. Broken, tilted or clogged heads create dry spots that homeowners fix by overwatering the rest.
  5. Watering dormant grass on a schedule. A brown, dormant lawn needs only about 0.25–0.5 inch every 2–4 weeks to stay alive, not the regular weekly amount.

How should watering change through the year?

The 1–1.5 inch target applies to the growing season. Grass needs far less in cool weather and a bit more at the peak of summer, so adjust the controller every month or two instead of leaving it on one program all year.

How can you tell when the lawn needs water?

You do not have to wait for brown. Three simple checks show thirst early:

  • Footprint test: walk across the lawn. If the blades spring back, it is fine. If footprints linger for several minutes, the grass has lost turgor and needs water.
  • Color: a blue-gray or dull tint is the first sign of drought stress, usually a day or two before wilting.
  • Soil probe: push a long screwdriver or soil probe into the ground. If it slides in easily to 6 inches, there is moisture; if it stops at 2–3 inches, the soil is dry.

Water as soon as you see the first sign. Grass recovers quickly from mild stress but struggles after it has gone fully brown.

Frequently asked questions

How many minutes should I water my lawn each day?

There is no single daily number. Work out the weekly inches (1–1.5) and divide by your sprinkler's output; for spray heads that is about 20–30 minutes, two to three times a week.

How do I know if I am overwatering my lawn?

Signs include spongy soil, mushrooms, yellowing grass, a heavy moss or algae layer and water running off the lawn. Probe the soil with a screwdriver: if it goes in with no effort 6 inches down a day after watering, you are likely giving too much.

Should I water my lawn after it rains?

Not until you have measured the rain. If a rain gauge shows about 0.5 inch or more, skip the next watering. Count rain toward the weekly 1–1.5 inch target.

Is it okay to water the lawn at night?

It is not ideal. Water sitting on the blades overnight can promote lawn diseases, so early morning is the better habit.

How long should I water new grass seed?

Keep the top inch of soil moist with brief watering, about 5–10 minutes once or twice a day, until germination. Then taper to less frequent, deeper watering over the next several weeks.

The bottom line

Give the lawn about 1 to 1.5 inches a week, in two or three early-morning sessions. Measure your sprinkler output with the can test, then divide inches needed by inches per hour. For quick answers by zone, use the irrigation run time calculator.
