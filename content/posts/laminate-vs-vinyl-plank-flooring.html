Quick answer

Choose vinyl plank (LVP) for kitchens, bathrooms, basements and rooms with pets or spills, since it's waterproof. Choose laminate for dry living areas where you want a harder, more wood-like surface. Both are floating floors you can install yourself. The deciding factors are moisture, noise and how you'll use the room.

These two floors look alike in the store and behave differently once they're down. Laminate has a wood-fiber core under a printed photographic layer. Vinyl plank is plastic all the way through, so it doesn't mind water the way a fiberboard core does. That one difference decides most of the choices below.

Key takeaways
  • Water: vinyl plank is waterproof; laminate is water-resistant at best and swells if water reaches the core.
  • Feel: laminate is harder and more rigid, vinyl is softer and slightly warmer underfoot.
  • Wear: check the laminate AC rating (AC3 and up for homes) and the vinyl wear layer in mils.
  • Install: both float over a flat subfloor with a small expansion gap at the walls.
  • Price: both span a wide range, from budget to premium, so compare actual products.

What is the difference between laminate and vinyl plank?

Laminate is made of layers: a clear wear layer on top, a photographic image of wood or stone, a high-density fiberboard (HDF) core and a backing. It's a wood-based product with a protective surface.

Vinyl plank (luxury vinyl plank, or LVP) is built from layers of PVC. Many current products have a rigid core, often labeled SPC (stone plastic composite) or WPC (wood plastic composite), while older flexible vinyl is thinner and bends more. Neither core type is wood fiberboard.

Which is better for water, kitchens and bathrooms?

Vinyl plank is the safer pick for wet rooms. A fiberboard core absorbs water at the edges and seams, then swells and ruins the plank, which doesn't flatten again once it dries. Many laminates now have water-resistant coatings, but their guarantees usually cover only short-term spills that you wipe up promptly.

Waterproof vinyl isn't a flood solution either. Water that gets through the joints can still reach the subfloor and grow mold. Fix leaks quickly, keep the expansion gap sealed according to the manufacturer's instructions where required and read the warranty for water limits before you buy.

Which wears better with kids and pets?

Laminate has a hard, abrasion-resistant surface, rated AC1 to AC5 under European-style classes. AC3 is the usual choice for homes, and AC4 or AC5 for busy spaces. It resists scratches from pet claws but can chip.

Vinyl plank wear layers are measured in mils (thousandths of an inch). Many residential products have wear layers in the range of roughly 6–20 mil, and thicker is generally better for busy households with pets. A thicker vinyl wear layer resists scuffs, but heavy furniture or sharp objects can dent it.

How do laminate and vinyl plank compare on cost?

Both come in budget, mid-range and premium versions, and the ranges overlap so much that cost alone rarely settles the question. Material prices per square foot differ by brand, thickness and wear layer, and installed price depends on your region.

To compare fairly, price the material per square foot after your waste factor, add underlayment if the product doesn't include it, and count trim, transition strips and removal of the old floor. The flooring calculator helps you compare boxes and cost between products.

How hard is it to install each one?

Both are click-lock floating floors you can install yourself over a clean, flat subfloor. You won't need nails or glue for most products, just a saw, a tapping block and a few spacers.

  1. Check the subfloor. Most manufacturers ask for flatness within about 3/16 inch over 10 feet. Vinyl, being thinner, can telegraph bumps and dips more than laminate.
  2. Acclimate the planks. Leave the unopened boxes in the room for the period listed in the instructions, usually around 48 hours, so they adjust to room conditions.
  3. Leave an expansion gap. Keep a gap, commonly about 1/4–1/2 inch, around the room's edges so the floor can move. Baseboards or trim cover it.
  4. Stagger the seams. Offset end joints by at least the distance in the instructions, often 6 inches or more.
  5. Cut with the right tool. Laminate dulls blades faster and needs a fine-tooth saw; vinyl can often be scored and snapped.

Always follow the manufacturer's installation instructions, since gaps, underlayment and acclimation rules differ by product and affect the warranty.

Which should you choose?

  • Choose vinyl plank for kitchens, bathrooms, basements, laundry rooms, mudrooms and homes with pets or small children.
  • Choose laminate for dry living areas and bedrooms where you want a harder, more wood-like feel, as long as the room isn't prone to spills or humidity.
  • Choose vinyl when you can't be sure the subfloor will stay dry, such as over a concrete slab.
  • Choose laminate when you want a stiff floor that doesn't flex over minor subfloor flaws.
Whichever you choose, don't skip the subfloor prep. Floating floors telegraph every dip and bump, and the locking edges can break if the floor flexes. Level high spots, fill low spots and fix moisture problems before the first plank goes down.

How do laminate and vinyl compare for sound and comfort?

Floating floors can sound hollow when walked on, and the type of underlayment makes a big difference. Laminate tends to be a bit louder because the fiberboard core is rigid and transmits impact noise, so a quality underlayment helps in upstairs rooms. Vinyl plank, especially with an attached pad, is typically quieter and slightly softer underfoot.

Temperature is another comfort factor. Vinyl can feel cooler over a concrete slab, and some rigid-core vinyl products have limits on indoor temperature swings, such as in sunrooms with large south-facing windows. Laminate is generally stable in temperature but reacts more to humidity. Read the manufacturer's limits for your room before buying.

If you're torn, bring home large samples of both and look at them in your own room's light. Drop a little water on each, scratch them with a key and step on them barefoot. Ten minutes with samples settles more questions than a spec sheet.

Frequently asked questions

Is vinyl plank more durable than laminate?

It depends on the use. Vinyl handles moisture better and tolerates impacts that would chip laminate, while laminate resists scratches better. For wet areas vinyl is more durable; for dry rooms they're close.

Can you put laminate in a bathroom?

It isn't recommended. Water that reaches the fiberboard core at seams or edges can make it swell permanently. Waterproof vinyl plank or tile is the safer choice for bathrooms.

Does vinyl plank flooring need underlayment?

Many products have an attached pad, and adding another layer can void the warranty. Check the instructions; laminate often needs a separate underlayment unless a pad is attached.

What does the AC rating on laminate mean?

AC stands for abrasion class, a scale from AC1 to AC5 that rates how well the surface resists wear. For homes, AC3 is a typical minimum, while AC4 and AC5 are for heavier traffic.

How thick should the wear layer on vinyl plank be?

Many residential products fall in the 6–20 mil range. A thicker layer lasts longer, so look for 12 mil or higher in busy homes or with pets, and check the manufacturer's residential warranty.

The bottom line

Pick vinyl plank where water is a factor and laminate for dry rooms where a harder feel matters. Prepare the subfloor carefully, follow the manufacturer's gap and acclimation rules and buy from one lot. The flooring calculator will tell you how many boxes you need before you commit.
