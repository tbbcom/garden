Quick answer

To winterize raised beds, clear spent plants, top up with 2 inches of compost, then cover the soil. Use 2–4 inches of shredded leaves or straw, a cover crop, or fall-planted garlic so the surface is never bare. Check the wood frame and drain hoses. A 4×8 bed needs about 5⅓ cubic feet of compost for a 2-inch layer.

Raised beds drain fast and warm early in spring, which also means they lose organic matter and nutrients faster when left bare in winter. Rain compacts exposed soil, weeds sprout in any gap and freeze-thaw cycles break down soil structure. A little work in fall gives you a bed that is ready to plant as soon as the soil thaws.

This routine takes about an hour per bed. If you are not sure how much material to buy, the soil calculator converts bed dimensions to cubic feet, cubic yards and bags in seconds.

Key takeaways
  • Never leave soil bare: cover it with mulch, a cover crop or a crop such as garlic.
  • Top-dress with compost: 2 inches adds 5.33 cu ft to a 4×8 bed, 2.67 cu ft to a 4×4 bed.
  • Mulch depth: 2–4 inches of shredded leaves or straw; 4 inches on a 4×8 bed is 10.67 cu ft.
  • Don't till or dig: turning frozen or wet soil damages structure; add material to the top instead.
  • Check the frame: tighten screws, replace rotted boards and clear gaps before ice forms.

When should you winterize raised garden beds?

Start after your main crops are finished and ideally four to six weeks before the ground freezes. That gives a cover crop time to establish and garlic time to root. In the northern US that means September to October; in zones 7–8, October to November. Use our frost dates by ZIP code tool to find your first fall frost, then count back.

What are the steps to winterize a raised bed?

Step 1: Clear spent plants and debris

Pull annuals and remove fallen fruit. Toss diseased material in the trash rather than a cool compost pile. For beans and peas, cut the tops and leave the roots in the soil to decompose.

Step 2: Pull weeds and harvest what's left

Weeds that set seed in fall create a carpet of seedlings in spring. Remove them now, and pick the last green tomatoes before frost. Root crops such as carrots and parsnips can stay under mulch and be harvested as needed.

Step 3: Check the soil level and top up

Raised-bed soil settles over a season as organic matter breaks down. If the surface has dropped, fill it back up with compost or a bed mix. As a rule, top-dress with 1–2 inches of compost each fall.

Step 4: Cover the soil

Choose one of these three options. Bare soil is the one thing to avoid.

  • Mulch: spread 2–4 inches of shredded leaves, straw or other weed-free organic mulch. It insulates, suppresses weeds and feeds worms. Keep it a couple of inches away from any plants that are still growing.
  • Cover crop: sow winter rye, oats or crimson clover in early fall to hold the soil and add organic matter; chop and turn under or cut and leave in spring.
  • Winter crops: garlic, kale and spinach all overwinter in many zones; garlic goes in about 2–4 weeks before the ground freezes.

Step 5: Add protection for tender crops (optional)

Low tunnels with hoops and row cover extend greens and carrots into winter in zones 5 and warmer. A heavier cover or a cold frame adds more protection. For hardy crops, row cover also keeps leaves clean and cuts moisture swings.

How do you care for the wood frame in winter?

Winter moisture, snow and freeze-thaw cycles are tough on frames. A 10-minute inspection avoids spring repairs.

  • Tighten loose screws and corner brackets; swelling wood can pop joints.
  • Replace boards that are soft or crumbly when you press a screwdriver into them.
  • Remove soil or mulch piled against any wood above the soil line, which holds moisture.
  • Leave untreated cedar, redwood and composite beds unsealed; they weather naturally.
  • Check for a gap between the bed and the ground where mice may nest, and fill it with gravel or hardware cloth.
Don't dig or walk on wet or frozen beds. Compaction in winter pushes air out of the soil and takes weeks to recover. Work from the paths and leave the surface to settle.

Should you amend raised bed soil in fall or spring?

Do both, with different materials. In fall add slow-to-break-down organic matter: compost, shredded leaves, aged manure. In spring, add fast-acting nutrients based on a soil test, because nitrogen from fertilizer applied in fall can wash out of a well-drained bed before plants can use it. Can you plant in raised beds over winter?

Yes, with the right crops. Garlic is planted in fall and harvested the following summer. Spinach, kale, mâche and some lettuces survive under row cover in cold climates, and carrots and beets sown in late summer can be left in the ground under mulch. In zones 8–10, cool-season vegetables grow all winter.

5 raised-bed winterizing mistakes

  1. Leaving soil bare. Rain compacts it and washes nutrients out.
  2. Tilling or digging deeply. It damages soil structure and brings up weed seeds.
  3. Mulching with whole oak leaves in a thick mat. They can pack together; shred them first.
  4. Using weedy straw or hay. Make sure the mulch is seed-free.
  5. Ignoring the frame. A small repair now saves replacing a board in spring.

What about raised beds in cold-winter regions?

In zones 3–5, soil in raised beds can freeze solid from November until March. That is normal and not a problem for a bed covered in mulch. Snow is a good insulator, so don't shovel it off the beds; pile it on instead. In zones 6–7, soil freezes and thaws repeatedly, so a thick mulch layer helps keep the temperature steady and prevents heaving of shallow-rooted plants and garlic. In zones 8–10, winter is a growing season for greens, peas and brassicas, and the main job is replacing nutrients with compost between crops.

Whatever your climate, the principle is the same: protect the soil surface, add organic matter and leave the soil structure alone.

What should you do with compost and tools at the end of the season?

Fall is also the best time to build your next year's supply. Pile shredded leaves and spent plant material in a bin so it can break down over winter, and you'll have free compost by spring. Empty and clean any watering cans, drain drip lines and bring in trellis netting, plant labels and cages so they do not weather. A quick note on what grew where helps you rotate crop families next year, which reduces disease and pest buildup in the same soil.

Frequently asked questions

Should I cover raised beds in winter?

Yes. A cover of mulch, a cover crop or winter vegetables protects the soil from erosion and compaction, prevents weeds and adds organic matter. A tarp also works but must be removed in spring.

Can I leave dead plants in my raised bed over winter?

Healthy plants can stay as a natural mulch, but remove diseased ones, since they can carry problems into next season. Chop and leave bean and pea roots in the soil.

Is it OK to put leaves in raised beds?

Yes. Shredded leaves are a free source of organic matter. Spread them 2–4 inches thick, or let them break down into the soil over winter.

Do raised beds freeze solid?

Raised beds freeze more deeply than in-ground soil because they are exposed on the sides. Mulch, snow cover and a thick layer of organic matter reduce how fast and how deep the freezing goes.

How much compost should I add to a raised bed in fall?

One to two inches is typical. That is 2.67–5.33 cubic feet for a 4×8 bed.

The bottom line

Clear the bed, top up with 1–2 inches of compost, cover the surface with mulch, a cover crop or garlic and give the frame a quick check. Use the soil calculator to buy the right amount, then let winter do its work.
