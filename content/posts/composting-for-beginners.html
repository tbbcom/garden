Quick answer

To start composting, build a pile or bin at least 3 × 3 × 3 feet and layer about 3 parts "browns" (dry leaves, cardboard) to 1 part "greens" (food scraps, grass clippings) by volume. Keep it as damp as a wrung-out sponge, turn it every week or two, and you'll have finished compost in roughly 2 to 6 months.

Composting turns kitchen scraps and yard waste into dark, crumbly material that feeds your soil. It cuts what you send to the curb, saves money on soil amendments and improves almost every kind of garden soil. It also looks more complicated than it is. The recipe comes down to three things: the right mix of ingredients, moisture and air.

This guide covers a simple backyard method, what to compost and what to leave out, how to fix common problems and how to use the finished compost. To work out how much compost your beds need, use the garden calculator or the soil calculator.

Key takeaways
  • Ratio: about 3 parts browns to 1 part greens by volume (a gardener's rule of thumb, around a 25–30:1 carbon-to-nitrogen balance).
  • Size: at least 3 × 3 × 3 ft (27 cubic feet) to heat up and break down efficiently.
  • Moisture: like a wrung-out sponge, with a drop or two when squeezed.
  • Air: turn every 1–2 weeks for hot compost, or monthly for slower compost.
  • Time: about 2–6 months actively managed, 6–12+ months if left alone.

What is compost and how does composting work?

Compost is organic matter that has been broken down by bacteria, fungi and small creatures such as worms and beetles into a stable, soil-like material. The microbes need four things: carbon (food and energy from browns), nitrogen (protein from greens), water and oxygen. Get the balance right and the pile warms to 130°F to 160°F as microbes work, which speeds decomposition and kills most weed seeds and disease organisms. Get it wrong and you get a soggy, smelly or dry, inactive pile.

What can you compost? Browns and greens

"Greens" are wet, nitrogen-rich materials. "Browns" are dry, carbon-rich materials. The color is a guide, not a rule.

What should you not compost?

  • Meat, fish, bones, dairy and greasy food: they attract rodents and flies and can smell.
  • Pet waste from cats and dogs: it can carry parasites and pathogens.
  • Diseased plants: unless your pile reliably hits hot temperatures, leave them out.
  • Weeds with mature seeds or aggressive roots such as bindweed or Bermuda grass.
  • Treated wood, coal or charcoal ash, and glossy paper.
  • Grass clippings, hay or manure treated with persistent herbicides. Some weed killers survive composting and damage tomatoes, beans and other garden plants. If you aren't sure where it came from, don't use it.

How do you start a compost pile step by step?

  1. Pick a spot. Choose a level, well-drained place in partial shade, close enough to the kitchen and garden that you'll actually use it. Keep it a few feet from fences and buildings.
  2. Choose a bin or go free-form. A bin made from pallets, wire mesh or a store-bought tumbler works. A simple pile is fine too. Aim for at least 3 × 3 × 3 feet (27 cubic feet).
  3. Start with a coarse base of twigs or straw, 4 to 6 inches deep, for airflow at the bottom.
  4. Layer browns and greens. Add about 3 buckets of browns for every 1 bucket of greens, in layers 2 to 4 inches thick. Mix as you go if you can.
  5. Moisten each layer until it feels like a wrung-out sponge.
  6. Cover with a lid, tarp or layer of browns to keep out flies and excess rain.
  7. Turn it with a fork every 1 to 2 weeks to add oxygen. Move the outside material to the center.
  8. Check moisture and temperature now and then. A compost thermometer is helpful but not required.

What size should a compost bin be?

A pile smaller than 3 feet on a side loses heat quickly and breaks down slowly. A pile larger than about 5 feet is hard to turn and can go without oxygen in the center. The sweet spot is 3 to 4 feet on each side.

Finished compost is much smaller than the raw pile. A 27 cubic foot pile often shrinks to about half that volume or less, so a single bin makes only a few cubic feet of compost per batch. Two or three bins let you add to one while another matures.

How do you know when compost is finished?

Finished compost is dark brown, crumbly and smells like a forest floor. You shouldn't be able to recognize the original ingredients, except for a few woody bits. Other signs:

  • The pile no longer heats up after turning.
  • The volume has dropped to about half or less.
  • A handful kept in a closed bag for a few days doesn't smell sour or like ammonia.

With hot, frequent turning, compost can be ready in 2 to 3 months. A slower, "cold" pile that you only add to occasionally takes 6 to 12 months or longer.

How do you use finished compost?

Compost is a soil amendment and a mulch, not a replacement for soil. Typical uses:

  • Vegetable beds: spread 1 to 2 inches over the surface and work it into the top 4 to 6 inches before planting.
  • Mulch: a 1 to 2 inch layer around plants feeds the soil and holds moisture.
  • Lawns: top-dress with a 1/4 to 1/2 inch layer.
  • Potting mix: use up to about 25 percent compost in a container mix.

Wash your hands and wear gloves when handling compost, and don't breathe the dust of dry, finished compost. If you have a weakened immune system or lung condition, wear a mask when turning a pile, since compost can release mold spores.

Frequently asked questions

Do I have to turn my compost pile?

No, but turning makes it faster. A pile turned every 1 to 2 weeks can finish in 2 to 3 months; one that is never turned can take a year or more.

Can I compost in winter?

Yes, but the pile cools and slows in cold weather. Keep adding scraps buried in the center, insulate with leaves or straw, and expect the pile to speed up again in spring.

Will a compost pile attract rats?

It can if you add meat, dairy or oily food, or leave scraps uncovered. Compost only plant scraps, bury them under browns and use a bin with a lid or fine mesh to keep rodents out.

Are coffee grounds and eggshells good for compost?

Coffee grounds are a green material rich in nitrogen, so mix them with browns. Eggshells are fine in small amounts but break down slowly unless crushed.

How long does compost take to be ready?

With the right mix, moisture and regular turning, compost is ready in about 2 to 6 months. A neglected cold pile takes 6 to 12 months or longer.

Can I use finished compost as potting soil?

Not by itself. Straight compost is too dense and rich for containers. Mix it into potting mix at up to about 25 percent.

The bottom line

Composting for beginners comes down to a pile of at least 3 × 3 × 3 feet, about 3 parts browns to 1 part greens, damp like a wrung-out sponge and turned every week or two.
