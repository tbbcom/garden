Quick answer

Most cool-season lawns need 2–3 lb of Kentucky bluegrass seed or 6–8 lb of tall fescue or ryegrass per 1,000 sq ft for a new lawn, and about half that to overseed. Warm-season lawns need less: roughly 1–2 lb of Bermuda or zoysia per 1,000 sq ft. Always check the bag label, then multiply by your lawn size.

Grass seed is sold by the pound, but lawns are measured in square feet, and seeding rates differ by a factor of ten between species. Kentucky bluegrass seed is tiny, so a pound has millions of seeds; tall fescue seed is much larger. That's why one rate can't fit every lawn.

This guide gives seeding rates for common grass types, shows how to convert them to pounds for your lawn, and covers the difference between starting from scratch and overseeding. To get the area right first, the garden calculator helps you work out square footage.

Key takeaways
  • Formula: pounds of seed = lawn area (sq ft) ÷ 1,000 × the rate per 1,000 sq ft.
  • New lawn rates: Kentucky bluegrass 2–3 lb, fine fescue 4–5 lb, tall fescue and ryegrass 6–8 lb per 1,000 sq ft.
  • Overseeding: use about half the new-lawn rate.
  • Warm-season grasses: Bermuda and zoysia about 1–2 lb; centipede only 0.25–0.5 lb per 1,000 sq ft.
  • Mixes: follow the bag; the label rate is built for that mix.

What is the difference between a new lawn and overseeding?

A new lawn starts from bare, prepared soil, so seed has to produce 100% of the turf. Use the full rate. Overseeding adds seed into an existing lawn to thicken it or fix thin patches. The existing grass fills in part of the area, so about half the rate is plenty. Going heavier just causes seedlings to compete and die off.

For small bare spots, a good starting point is the new-lawn rate, since there is no grass there to help.

How do you spread grass seed evenly?

  1. Prepare the soil. For a new lawn, loosen the top 4–6 inches, remove rocks and rake smooth. For overseeding, mow low and rake out dead grass so seed touches soil.
  2. Measure and weigh. Calculate your pounds and set aside half for the first pass. A cheap kitchen or luggage scale works.
  3. Set the spreader. Use the setting on the bag, then spread half the seed walking one direction.
  4. Spread the second half crosswise. A perpendicular pass fills gaps.
  5. Rake in lightly so seed is about 1/4 inch deep. Most grass seed needs light and soil contact to germinate.
  6. Roll or press, then mulch. A thin layer of straw (about 1 bale per 1,000 sq ft, with seed still visible) holds moisture. Water lightly several times a day until seedlings are up.

What do the numbers on a grass seed bag mean?

Look at the label's "analysis" panel. It lists the percentage of each variety, pure seed, inert matter, weed seed and germination, plus a test date. Good seed has at least 85% germination, under 0.5% weed seed and a test date within the last year. Cheap mixes often contain a high percentage of annual ryegrass or "other crop," which is a fast cover but not a lasting lawn.

If the bag is coated seed, its weight includes the coating, so you may need to apply more by weight than the uncoated figure. Check the bag's coverage statement.

What seeding mistakes waste seed and money?

Don't put down crabgrass preventer at the same time as seed. Most pre-emergent herbicides stop grass seed from sprouting too. Wait until after the new grass has been mowed several times, or use a product labeled safe for new seedlings.
  1. Seeding at the wrong time. Seed sprouts best when soil temperatures are right: for cool-season grass, early fall; for warm-season grass, late spring.
  2. Using too much seed. Overcrowded seedlings are thin and prone to disease.
  3. Burying seed too deep. More than 1/4 to 1/2 inch of soil on top keeps many seeds from emerging.
  4. Letting it dry out. Seed that begins to germinate and then dries dies. Keep the top inch moist.
  5. Buying by price, not label. A $20 bag with 50% filler covers less than a $30 bag of pure seed.

Should you use seed, sod or plugs?

Seed is the cheapest way to start a lawn, but it needs the most patience and the most watering. Sod gives an instant lawn but costs several times more per square foot. Plugs and sprigs are common for warm-season grasses that spread by runners, such as Bermuda, zoysia and St. Augustine.

How do you care for new grass seedlings?

The first six weeks decide the lawn. Keep the seedbed moist but not soggy, then taper as roots deepen.

  • Days 1–14: water lightly two or three times a day to keep the top inch damp. Do not let it dry out.
  • Weeks 3–4: cut back to once a day or every other day with longer sessions as seedlings appear.
  • First mowing: when grass reaches about 3–4 inches, with a sharp blade, removing no more than one third of the height.
  • After four mowings: switch to normal deep, infrequent watering and apply a regular fertilizer if the schedule calls for it.
  • Foot traffic: keep it off the area as much as possible until the lawn has been mowed several times.

Unused grass seed stays viable for roughly a year or two if kept cool and dry. Check the test date on the label and avoid seed that has been stored in a hot garage.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a 50 lb bag of grass seed cover?

At 6–8 lb per 1,000 sq ft (tall fescue or ryegrass), a 50 lb bag seeds about 6,000–8,000 sq ft of new lawn, or roughly 12,500–16,500 sq ft when overseeding at 3–4 lb.

Can I put too much grass seed down?

Yes. Excess seed crowds seedlings, producing weak, shallow-rooted grass that is prone to fungus. Stay within the rate on the bag.

How many grass seeds are in a pound?

It varies widely: roughly 2 million for Kentucky bluegrass, about 250,000 for tall fescue and around 230,000 for perennial ryegrass. This is why the pounds-per-1,000-sq-ft rates differ so much.

Should I cover grass seed with topsoil or straw?

A thin layer of straw or peat holds moisture and helps seed stay in place. If you use topsoil, keep it to 1/8–1/4 inch so seed isn't buried.

Will grass seed grow if I just throw it on the lawn?

Rarely well. Seed that lands on thatch or dense grass doesn't touch soil. Mow low, rake the surface and press seed in for much better results.

The bottom line

Pick your grass, find its rate per 1,000 sq ft, and multiply by your lawn area. For a new lawn, use the full rate; for overseeding, use about half. Measure carefully, split the seed into two passes and keep it moist until it comes up. The garden calculator helps with the area step.
