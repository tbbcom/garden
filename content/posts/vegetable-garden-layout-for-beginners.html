Quick answer

A good beginner vegetable garden layout uses beds 3 to 4 feet wide, 2 to 3 feet apart, in a spot with at least 6 to 8 hours of direct sun and a water source nearby. Start with 32 to 100 square feet (one 4×8 bed up to two 4×10 beds), put tall crops on the north side and group plants by water needs.

The first year of a vegetable garden usually goes wrong for the same reasons: too much space in too much shade, rows so far apart you spend half your time weeding, and a bed so wide you can't reach the middle. A layout fixes these before you buy a single seed.

This guide walks through choosing a site, deciding bed size and path width, and three complete layouts you can copy, from a single 4 × 8 bed to a 10 × 10 plot. When you're ready to fill your plan, the plant spacing calculator tells you how many plants each bed holds.

Key takeaways
  • Sun: 6–8 hours of direct light; 8+ for tomatoes, peppers and squash.
  • Bed width: 3–4 ft so you can reach the middle from either side.
  • Path width: at least 18 in between beds, 3 ft where a wheelbarrow must pass.
  • Start small: 32–100 sq ft is plenty for a first season.
  • North side for tall crops, so tomatoes and trellises don't shade shorter plants.

Where should you put a vegetable garden?

Site matters more than any other layout decision. Walk your yard at 9 a.m., noon and 4 p.m. and note where the sun lands. Then check these four things:

  • Sun: most vegetables need 6 to 8 hours of direct sun. Leafy greens, peas and herbs tolerate 4 to 6 hours of sun, while fruiting crops like tomatoes, peppers and squash want 8.
  • Water: put the garden within hose reach. If you have to carry water, you will water less often.
  • Drainage: avoid low spots where water pools after rain. Soil should drain within a few hours of a heavy rain.
  • Distance from trees: tree roots steal water and nutrients, and shade ruins yields. Stay at least as far as the tree's drip line, and further from black walnuts.

Also think about convenience. A garden you pass on the way to the car gets visited every day, which means more weeds pulled and more ripe tomatoes picked.

For most beginners, raised or in-ground beds 3 to 4 feet wide beat traditional rows. You walk only on the paths, so the planted soil stays loose, and you can plant closer together because there's no wasted aisle between every row.

How wide should beds and paths be?

Beds should be narrow enough to reach the middle without stepping in, which is usually half the width of your arm span plus a little. That works out to 3 feet for most people and 4 feet if you're tall or have long arms. Length is flexible, but 8 to 12 feet is easy to manage.

Path widths depend on what has to fit:

  • 18 to 24 inches: walking and kneeling in a small garden.
  • 30 to 36 inches: wheelbarrow and comfortable access, the best width for main paths.
  • 4 feet: wheelchair access or space for a garden cart.

Mulch paths with wood chips, straw or gravel over cardboard. They suppress weeds and prevent mud, and the mulch calculator shows how much to buy for a path of any length.

What does a beginner vegetable garden layout look like? Three plans

All three plans keep the tall crops on the north side and group plants by size. Planting areas are calculated from the bed sizes.

Plan 1: One bed, 32 sq ft (4 × 8 ft)

This is the right size for a first year. Put two staked tomatoes at the north end, a block of peppers beside them, bush beans and lettuce in the middle, and carrots and radishes in the south end. Add a row of marigolds or herbs along the edge. The plant spacing calculator gives exact plant counts for each crop.

Plan 2: Two beds, 64 sq ft (two 4 × 8 ft beds)

Place two beds side by side with a 3-foot path between them. The footprint is 4 + 3 + 4 = 11 feet wide by 8 feet long, or 88 sq ft in total, with 64 sq ft (73%) planted. Put warm-season crops (tomatoes, peppers, squash) in one bed and cool-season crops (lettuce, peas, broccoli) in the other. The second bed makes crop rotation easy next year.

Plan 3: 10 × 10 ft plot, 80 sq ft planted

Divide a 10 × 10 square into two 4 × 10 beds with a 2-foot center path: 4 + 2 + 4 = 10 feet. Planted area is 2 × 40 = 80 sq ft, or 80% of the plot. Put a trellis along the north edge for cucumbers or pole beans, a compost bin just outside the plot and a hose bib on the south end.

How do you decide what goes where?

  1. Put tall crops north. Tomatoes, pole beans, corn and trellised cucumbers throw shade; keep them on the north side (south in the Southern Hemisphere).
  2. Group by water needs. Lettuce and cucumbers like steady moisture; tomatoes and peppers prefer even but less frequent watering; herbs like rosemary and thyme prefer it dry.
  3. Plant fast and slow crops together. Radishes (about 25 days) fill the gaps around slow crops like peppers (about 70+ days from transplant).
  4. Keep perennials separate. Asparagus, rhubarb and berries live for years; give them their own corner so you don't till them up.
  5. Leave room for succession. Save a space for a second planting of beans, lettuce or fall carrots. Check dates in the planting calendar.

What should a beginner grow first?

Pick crops you actually eat that are forgiving to grow: lettuce, radishes, bush beans, zucchini, cherry tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers, carrots and herbs such as basil, chives and parsley. Skip the fussy ones (cauliflower, celery, melons in short-season areas) until you've had a good season or two.

A good beginner rule is to plant two or three vegetables you love for every one you're curious about. Also, favor varieties labeled for your region, since they are bred for local pests, disease and day length.

What mistakes should you avoid in the first season?

  1. Starting too big. A 20 × 30 plot that you can't keep weeded is worse than a 4 × 8 bed that thrives. Add a bed next year.
  2. Planting in shade. Fewer than 6 hours of sun means small harvests, whatever else you do right.
  3. Making beds too wide. Stepping on the soil compacts it and harms roots.
  4. Forgetting water access. Install a hose, soaker line or drip irrigation from day one.
  5. Overcrowding. Plant to mature size, not seedling size, or plants shade and crowd each other.
  6. Skipping labels. Mark each variety and sowing date. You'll forget by July.
Test the soil before you plant. If your garden is on a former lawn, near an older house or beside a busy road, a soil test checks nutrients and lead. A state extension service can run one for a small fee, and results come with fertilizer recommendations.

Frequently asked questions

How big should my first vegetable garden be?

A first garden of 32 to 100 square feet (one 4 × 8 bed up to a 10 × 10 plot) is plenty. It grows a surprising amount and takes only a few hours a week to maintain.

Is it better to plant vegetables in rows or beds?

Beds are better for most home gardens. They allow closer spacing, cut the walking area and keep soil loose because you never step on it. Rows suit very large gardens or crops like corn and potatoes.

Which direction should a vegetable garden face?

Choose a site that gets full sun from the south, and run beds north to south if you can so both sides get equal light. Put tall crops on the north end so they don't shade shorter ones.

How far apart should raised beds be?

Allow 2 to 3 feet between beds. Two feet works for walking, while 3 feet leaves room for a wheelbarrow and kneeling.

How many hours a week does a vegetable garden take?

A 4 × 8 bed typically takes 1 to 3 hours a week once it is established, mostly for watering, weeding and harvesting. Planting weeks and mid-summer harvest weeks take longer.

Should I plant in the same spot every year?

Rotate crop families to a different bed or area every year if you can. Tomatoes, peppers and potatoes shouldn't follow each other, because they share soil-borne diseases.

The bottom line

A beginner-friendly vegetable garden layout is simple: a sunny, level site near water, beds 3 to 4 feet wide, paths of 2 to 3 feet and tall crops on the north side. Start with one or two 4 × 8 beds, and add more once you know what you will actually eat. Use the plant spacing calculator to turn your plan into a shopping list.
