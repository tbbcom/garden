Quick answer

Plant strawberries in early spring, as soon as the soil can be worked, roughly 4–6 weeks before your last frost in zones 3–7. In zones 8–10, plant in fall (about October to November) and grow them as a cool-season crop. Dormant bare-root plants go in first; potted plants can follow later.

Strawberries are forgiving about the exact day but not about the season. Plant too late in spring and the crowns face summer heat before their roots are established. Plant too early in fall in a cold climate and frost heaving can push them out of the ground. Matching the planting window to your climate is most of the battle.

This guide gives spring and fall planting dates for USDA zones 3–10, explains how June-bearing, everbearing and day-neutral types differ, and walks through planting bare-root crowns. To pin down your own dates, check your frost dates by ZIP code, then find your exact window in the planting calendar.

Key takeaways
  • Cold zones (3–7): plant in spring, from about 5 weeks before to the day of your average last frost, once soil is workable.
  • Warm zones (8–10): plant in fall; summer heat makes spring-planted crowns struggle.
  • Depth: the crown (where the leaves meet the roots) must sit exactly level with the soil surface.
  • Spacing: 12–24 in apart depending on growing system; a 4×8 ft bed on a 12-inch grid holds 32 plants.
  • First year: pinch off flowers on June-bearing plants; this builds a stronger plant and bigger crops the following year.

What is the best time of year to plant strawberries?

For most of the country the answer is early spring. Strawberries are cool-season perennials that root best in cool, moist soil, and they need the whole growing season to build crowns that will bear well. Bare-root plants are shipped dormant in late winter and early spring for this reason, and they should go into the ground within a few days of arriving.

The exception is the Deep South, the Gulf Coast, southern California and similar mild-winter areas. There, plants set out in fall grow through the mild winter and fruit in late winter and spring before summer heat arrives, and many gardeners treat them as annuals. Extension guidance for the Southeast and Southwest generally follows this fall pattern.

Fall planting in cold climates is possible with potted plants set out very early (late summer), but most northern extension services favor spring because winter heaving and cold injury threaten young plants.

Do June-bearing, everbearing and day-neutral strawberries need different timing?

The planting window is similar, but the type you choose changes what you do afterward and when you harvest.

Spacing varies by source and system, so treat the table as a range and check the tag that comes with your plants. June-bearing plants send out many runners, which fill in a row (the matted-row system). Everbearing and day-neutral types make fewer runners and do well in tight beds, containers and raised beds.

How do you plant bare-root strawberries step by step?

  1. Prepare the bed ahead of time. Choose a spot with 6–8 hours of full sun and well-drained soil. Strawberries prefer a slightly acidic soil pH of about 5.8–6.5. Work in 2–3 inches of compost.
  2. Avoid recent tomato, pepper, potato and eggplant beds. These crops can leave behind verticillium wilt in the soil, which attacks strawberries. Wait a few years or choose another bed.
  3. Unpack and soak. Soak bare-root plants in a bucket of lukewarm water for 20 minutes to an hour, no longer. Trim any roots longer than about 8 inches.
  4. Dig a hole wide enough to spread the roots out fanned downward rather than curled up in a "J".
  5. Set the crown level with the soil surface. The crown is the thick base where the leaves emerge. Roots covered by soil, crown fully visible.
  6. Firm the soil and water well to settle it around the roots.
  7. Mulch lightly with 1–2 inches of straw once plants are growing, keeping the crowns clear.

A 4 × 8 ft raised bed (32 sq ft) planted on a 12-inch grid holds 32 plants. A 100-foot matted row planted 18 inches apart holds about 67 plants.

Crown depth makes or breaks the plant. If the crown is buried, it rots. If it sits above the soil with roots exposed, the roots dry out. Get it level with the soil surface, and recheck after the first heavy rain, which can settle the soil.

What if you plant strawberries at the wrong time?

Strawberries are tough, but timing mistakes reduce yields. Planting in late spring or summer in a hot climate leaves plants with a weak root system just as heat and drought hit. If you must plant late, choose potted plants, shade them for the first week and water deeply.

Planting too early in fall in a cold zone means plants grow lots of top growth that freezes. Planting too late in fall gives roots no time to establish before the ground freezes, and heaving can lift them. A 2–3 inch layer of straw after the ground freezes helps in cold areas; remove it gradually in spring as growth resumes.

A late frost after spring planting is less of a worry for dormant plants, but open blossoms are tender. If a frost or freeze is forecast when plants are in bloom, cover them overnight with a frost blanket or row cover.

What are the most common strawberry planting mistakes?

  1. Burying the crown. This is the number-one cause of failed transplants.
  2. Letting bare roots dry out. Keep them moist and covered from unpacking to planting.
  3. Letting first-year plants fruit. Early fruiting drains the plant. Remove flowers on June-bearing plants the first season; on everbearing and day-neutral types, remove flowers until about early July.
  4. Planting too thick. Crowded plants get gray mold and leaf spot because air cannot move through them.
  5. Using a bed with heavy weed pressure. Strawberries are poor competitors. Clear perennial weeds first, because pulling them later disturbs the shallow roots.

Frequently asked questions

Can you plant strawberries in summer?

You can, but it is the hardest season. Heat and dry spells stress young plants, so use potted plants, water deeply, and provide afternoon shade for the first week or two. Fall or spring planting is more reliable.

How long until new strawberry plants produce fruit?

June-bearing plants usually give their first real harvest the spring after planting, about a year later. Everbearing and day-neutral plants set in spring can produce a modest crop in late summer or fall of the same year if early flowers were removed.

Should you pinch off strawberry blossoms the first year?

Yes for June-bearing types: remove all flowers in year one so the plant puts energy into roots and runners. For everbearing and day-neutral types, pinch flowers until about early July, then let them fruit.

How deep should bare-root strawberries be planted?

Plant them so the roots are fully covered and the crown sits level with the soil surface. The crown is the thick base from which the leaves grow, and it must never be buried.

How far apart should you plant strawberries?

Space June-bearing plants 18–24 inches apart in rows 3–4 feet apart if you want a matted row. Space everbearing and day-neutral plants 8–12 inches apart in a hill system or raised bed.

Will a spring frost kill strawberry blossoms?

Open blossoms and young fruit are damaged by temperatures around freezing, though dormant plants and leaves tolerate cold well. Cover plants with a row cover or frost blanket the night a frost is forecast and uncover them in the morning.

The bottom line

Plant strawberries in early spring in zones 3–7, from about 5 weeks before your last frost until the frost date itself, and in fall in zones 8–10.
