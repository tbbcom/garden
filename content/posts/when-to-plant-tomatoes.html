Quick answer

Plant tomato transplants outdoors 1–2 weeks after your average last spring frost, when nights stay above 50°F and the soil is at least 60°F. Start seeds indoors 6–8 weeks before that date. Most gardeners in zones 5–7 plant between late April and mid-May.

Tomatoes are the crop gardeners most often put out too soon. The plants look ready in April, the garden center is full of them, and then one cold night turns the leaves black. The good news is that the right date comes from one number: your average last frost.

This guide gives you tomato-specific dates for USDA zones 3 through 10, the soil temperature that really matters, and how to handle fall crops in the South. To get dates for your exact location, find your frost dates by ZIP code, then drop them into the planting calendar.

Key takeaways
  • Transplant date: 1–2 weeks after the average last frost, never before nighttime lows hold above 50°F.
  • Soil temperature: at least 60°F at 4 inches deep; 70–85°F is ideal for root growth.
  • Seed starting: 6–8 weeks before the last frost date, indoors under lights.
  • Direct sowing: only works in long, warm seasons (zones 8–10); elsewhere use transplants.
  • Harvest: about 60–85 days from transplanting for most varieties; 55–60 days for the earliest types.

When is the best time to plant tomatoes?

Tomatoes are frost-tender and heat-loving. A light frost at 32°F kills the plant outright, and even temperatures in the 40s stunt growth and cause leaves to turn purple. For this reason the standard extension advice is to wait until 1–2 weeks after the average last spring frost.

The "average" last frost date means there is still about a 50% chance of a later frost in some years. Waiting the extra week or two covers that risk and gives the soil time to warm. If you plant earlier, be ready to cover plants overnight with row cover, buckets or frost cloth.

What soil and air temperature do tomatoes need?

Calendar dates are a shortcut. The plant cares about temperature, and you can check it directly.

Push a soil thermometer 4 inches into the bed in the morning. If it reads under 60°F, wait a few days or warm the soil with black plastic mulch for one to two weeks before planting.

Can you plant tomatoes in fall in warm climates?

In zones 9 and 10, summer heat is the problem rather than frost. When daytime highs stay above 90°F, tomatoes drop blossoms and fruit set stops. Southern gardeners often get two seasons: an early spring crop planted from mid-February to March, and a fall crop planted in late summer so fruit sets as nights cool. For the fall crop, count back roughly 90–100 days from your first fall frost (December 1 in zone 9, December 20 in zone 10) to a planting date in late August or early September, and give seedlings afternoon shade while they establish.

In the rest of the country, frost ends the season well before a second crop could ripen, so one planting in spring is the rule.

Should you start tomatoes from seed or buy transplants?

Both work, but the timing is different.

  • Seed indoors: sow 6–8 weeks before the last frost in a warm spot (70–80°F) under lights. Older, bigger plants than 8 weeks tend to get leggy and root-bound.
  • Buy transplants: look for stocky plants about 6–10 inches tall with dark green leaves, and no flowers or fruit yet. Do not buy the biggest plant in the flat.
  • Direct sow outdoors: tomato seed needs soil of at least 60°F to germinate well and a long season. This works in zones 9–10 but is risky elsewhere, as it adds three to four weeks to the wait for fruit.

Tomato varieties differ in how long they take to ripen. Short-season types such as many cherry tomatoes and early slicers can ripen in 55–70 days from transplanting. Large beefsteak and heirloom types usually need 80–90 days or more. In zones 3–4, choose short-season varieties so fruit ripens before fall frost.

How do you plant tomato transplants step by step?

  1. Harden off the plants. For 7–10 days, set them outdoors in shade for a few hours, then lengthen the time and sunlight each day.
  2. Prepare the bed. Pick a spot with 6–8 hours of full sun and well-drained soil. Mix in 2–3 inches of compost.
  3. Dig the hole deep. Tomatoes grow roots along buried stem, so plant them deeper than they were in the pot.
  4. Remove the lowest leaves and bury the stem up to the first set of true leaves. For a leggy plant, lay the stem sideways in a shallow trench and bend the top upward.
  5. Space plants 18–24 inches apart for staked or caged plants, and 24–36 inches for sprawling types. Use the plant spacing calculator to plan the bed.
  6. Water deeply at planting, then add 2–3 inches of straw or other mulch once the soil is warm.
  7. Add a cage or stake at planting time, so you do not damage roots later.

Which tomato planting mistakes should you avoid?

Do not plant on the first warm weekend. A stretch of 75°F days in March or April is common, and a freeze often follows. Check the 10-day forecast for lows under 40°F before planting.
  1. Planting before the soil is 60°F. Cold soil slows roots even if the air feels warm.
  2. Skipping hardening off. Indoor-grown plants get sunburned and wind-damaged in a single day outside.
  3. Buying plants already in bloom. They stop growing to make fruit. Pinch off early flowers so the plant builds roots first.
  4. Planting too shallow. Roots on buried stem make the plant sturdier and more drought tolerant.
  5. Crowding plants. Poor airflow increases leaf diseases such as early blight and septoria leaf spot.
  6. Planting in the same spot every year. Rotate away from tomatoes, peppers, potatoes and eggplant for about three years to reduce soilborne disease.

How can you plant tomatoes earlier safely?

If you want fruit a little sooner, you can plant up to two weeks before the last frost, but only with protection. Warm the soil first with black plastic or clear plastic for 1–2 weeks. Use wall-of-water sleeves, cloches or a low tunnel, and keep row cover on hand for nights forecast under 40°F. Choose early varieties. Expect some risk: cold weather can delay plants more than it helps them, and a patient planting often catches up with an early one.

Frequently asked questions

What month should I plant tomatoes?

It depends on zone: April or May for most of the country, late May to June in zones 3–4, and February or March in zones 8–10. The rule is 1–2 weeks after your last spring frost.

Is it too late to plant tomatoes in July?

Not in warm climates. Count 60–90 days to harvest from planting and make sure that lands before your first fall frost. In zones 5–6 a July planting of a short-season variety can still work, but zones 3–4 are usually too late.

How cold is too cold for tomato transplants?

Anything near 32°F kills them, and nights below about 50°F stall growth. Cover plants whenever lows are forecast under 40°F.

How many weeks before the last frost should I start tomato seeds?

Six to eight weeks. Starting earlier produces tall, root-bound seedlings that struggle after transplanting.

How long do tomatoes take to ripen after planting?

Most varieties ripen 60–85 days after transplanting. Early cherry types can take about 55–65 days, while large heirlooms may need 90 days or more.
