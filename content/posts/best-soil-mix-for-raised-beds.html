Quick answer

A simple, proven raised bed soil mix is about 60% topsoil, 30% compost and 10% coarse aggregate like perlite or pumice, by volume. A 4 × 8 ft bed 12 inches deep (32 cu ft) needs about 19 cu ft topsoil, 10 cu ft compost and 3 cu ft perlite. Mix on a tarp, fill the bed and water to settle it.

Raised beds are only as good as what is in them. The wrong blend dries out in a week, turns to concrete after rain, or sinks by half by midsummer. The right blend holds moisture, drains freely and feeds plants for the season.

Below are three common recipes, the ratios converted into cubic feet for common beds, and what to avoid. The soil calculator tells you how much total volume to buy first.

Key takeaways
  • Budget mix: 60% topsoil, 30% compost, 10% perlite or pumice by volume.
  • Lightweight mix: equal parts compost, peat or coir, and vermiculite (the "Mel's Mix" style).
  • Compost limit: keep compost at roughly 30–50% of the mix; more shrinks fast and can be too rich.
  • For a 4 × 8 ft bed at 12 in: 32 cu ft total; add 10% for settling.
  • Avoid: fill dirt, pure potting mix, sand mixed into clay and untreated manure.

What should you put in a raised bed?

A good raised bed mix does three jobs: hold water, drain, and supply nutrients. Mineral soil (topsoil) gives body and holds nutrients. Compost feeds microbes and plants. A coarse, light ingredient adds air space so roots can breathe. Everything else is a variation on those three.

The blends below follow the general advice of US university extension services, which commonly recommend mixing topsoil with a generous proportion of finished compost rather than using either alone.

Mix A is the one most gardeners should start with. Mix B is light and fluffy, but expensive to fill a large bed, and the peat in it is a non-renewable resource, which is why many gardeners prefer coir. Mix C gives heavy soils more air.

How much does each mix cost to make?

Using example prices (not quotes) of $35 per cubic yard for bulk topsoil, $40 per cubic yard for bulk compost and $15 for a 2 cu ft bag of perlite, Mix A for a 4 × 8 ft bed at 12 inches costs about:

  • Topsoil: 0.71 yd × $35 = $25
  • Compost: 0.36 yd × $40 = $14
  • Perlite: 2 bags × $15 = $30
  • Total: about $69 before delivery, against roughly $132 for 22 bags of ready-made mix at $6 per 1.5 cu ft.

Perlite is the surprise cost. You can cut the bill by using less aggregate (down to 5%), or by skipping it if your topsoil is already sandy and drains well. Delivery fees apply to bulk loads, so it makes sense for two or more beds.

How do you mix and fill a raised bed?

  1. Loosen the ground under the bed with a garden fork so roots and water can move down.
  2. Lay the ingredients on a tarp in the right ratio. A 5-gallon bucket (0.67 cu ft) is a handy unit: Mix A for a 32 cu ft bed is about 29 buckets of topsoil, 14 of compost and 5 of perlite.
  3. Mix with a shovel until it looks uniform, with no pockets of pure compost.
  4. Fill in layers of 4–6 inches and water each lightly to settle.
  5. Leave 1–2 inches below the rim.
  6. Top up with compost after the first month if the bed has sunk.

Which ingredients are worth the money?

  • Topsoil: a screened, weed-free product from a landscape supplier is much better than bags of cheap "fill". Ask for a sample and feel it: it should crumble, not form a hard ball or stay powdery.
  • Compost: use finished, dark, earthy-smelling compost. A mix of sources (yard waste, composted manure, mushroom compost) gives broader nutrients. Salt-heavy compost can hurt seedlings, so check the supplier.
  • Perlite or pumice: improves drainage and air. Coarse sand is a poor substitute, since sand mixed into clay can set like concrete; if used, it must be coarse and a large share.
  • Peat or coir: hold water and keep the mix light. Coir is more sustainable; peat is acidic, so a mix with a lot of peat may need lime.
  • Vermiculite: holds water and nutrients; mostly used in the lightweight mix.
Don't fill with cheap fill dirt or pure potting soil. Fill dirt is subsoil with almost no organic matter. Potting mix is designed for pots and can shrink and dry fast in a large bed. Avoid raw manure and any compost with unknown herbicide residue.

How do you check and adjust your mix?

Most vegetables grow best at a soil pH of about 6.0–7.0. A simple lab test from your state extension service or a commercial lab checks pH, nutrient levels and organic matter. Test before adding lime or sulfur, which can do harm if applied blindly. Also do a water check: after a good soaking, the bed should drain within a few hours without standing water, and the soil should stay moist about an inch down.

Each spring, add 1–2 inches of fresh compost on top and rake it in lightly. Over the years the soil level drops as organic matter breaks down; topping up restores both volume and fertility.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use only compost in a raised bed?

Pure compost is usually too rich and fluffy, and it shrinks quickly. Most guidance recommends keeping compost at roughly 30–50% of the mix and blending it with topsoil.

Can I use potting soil in a raised bed?

You can, but it is expensive in bulk and dries out and shrinks quickly. It works better as an amendment for part of the mix or for containers than as the whole fill.

Should I add sand to my raised bed soil?

Generally no. Fine sand mixed with clay can make a dense, concrete-like soil. Perlite or pumice improve drainage more safely.

What is Mel's Mix?

Mel's Mix is a popular lightweight recipe from the Square Foot Gardening method: equal parts compost, peat moss (or coconut coir) and vermiculite. It is light and holds water but costs more than a topsoil-based blend.

How often should I replace raised bed soil?

You rarely need to replace it. Add 1–2 inches of compost each year and rotate crops. Replace only if the soil has disease problems, is contaminated or has broken down to almost nothing.

Do I need to fill the bottom of the bed with branches or rocks?

No. Rocks at the bottom do not improve drainage. A layer of logs or branches can reduce the amount of soil you need but they break down and the bed will settle.

The bottom line

A mix of about 60% topsoil, 30% compost and 10% perlite suits most raised vegetable beds, and a 4 × 8 ft bed at 12 inches needs 32 cubic feet in total. Add 10% for settling, top up with compost each spring, and use the soil calculator to get your totals.
