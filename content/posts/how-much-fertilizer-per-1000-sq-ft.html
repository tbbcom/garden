Quick answer

Most lawn feedings call for 0.5 to 1 pound of actual nitrogen per 1,000 sq ft. To find pounds of product, divide 100 by the nitrogen percentage on the bag: a 20% nitrogen fertilizer takes 5 lb per 1,000 sq ft to deliver 1 lb of nitrogen, and a 30% product takes about 3.3 lb.

The most common fertilizer mistake isn't picking the wrong product; it's putting down the wrong amount. The bag's rate is written for that specific bag, but if you switch brands, buy a different grade or treat an odd-shaped yard, you need to work it out yourself. The math is simpler than it looks, and getting it right protects both your lawn and local waterways.

This guide gives the one formula you need, a chart of product amounts by nitrogen percentage, and worked examples for common lawn sizes. For instant answers, use the lawn fertilizer NPK calculator.

Key takeaways
  • Formula: pounds of product per 1,000 sq ft = pounds of N wanted × 100 ÷ nitrogen % on the bag.
  • Standard dose: 0.5–1 lb of nitrogen per 1,000 sq ft per feeding.
  • Upper limit: no more than 1 lb of quick-release nitrogen per 1,000 sq ft at a time.
  • Bag coverage: a 50 lb bag of 20% nitrogen covers 10,000 sq ft at 1 lb N per 1,000.
  • Measure first: wrong square footage causes most over- and under-application.

How do you calculate fertilizer per 1,000 square feet?

Fertilizer rates for lawns are set in pounds of nitrogen, not pounds of product, because nitrogen is what drives growth and what can burn grass. The product is only a carrier for that nitrogen. So the calculation has three steps.

Step 1: Choose pounds of nitrogen per 1,000 sq ft

For most feedings, pick 1 lb (or 0.5 lb for slow-release light feedings and for warm-season grasses in summer). Your extension service's schedule for your grass type gives the exact number; the calculator uses the same inputs.

Step 2: Divide by the nitrogen percentage

Take pounds of nitrogen wanted, multiply by 100, and divide by the first number on the bag.

Example: bag is 24-0-6, wanted 1 lb N: 1 × 100 ÷ 24 = 4.17 lb of product per 1,000 sq ft.

Step 3: Multiply by your lawn size in thousands

A 5,000 sq ft lawn is five "thousands." So 4.17 × 5 = 20.8 lb of 24-0-6, about 21 lb for the whole lawn.

How do you measure the lawn area to get this right?

Don't use the lot size from the listing; it includes the house, driveway and beds. Measure only the grass. Pace off or tape each section, multiply length × width for rectangles, and add them. Subtract the house, driveway, patios and planting beds. If you're unsure, an online map measuring tool or your county's GIS parcel map is close enough. The garden calculator can also help with area.

How do you apply fertilizer evenly?

  1. Weigh the product. Weigh out the pounds you calculated rather than relying on the spreader dial alone.
  2. Set the spreader. Start with the dial setting printed on the bag, which is built for that product.
  3. Apply half, then the other half. Split the product, spread the first half in one direction and the second half crosswise.
  4. Close the hopper at turns, and shut it off before stopping. Spreaders drop a pile where they sit still.
  5. Sweep strays off driveways, sidewalks and into the lawn so product doesn't wash into storm drains.
  6. Water it in lightly if the label directs.

What are the most common fertilizer-rate mistakes?

The bag's label rate is a maximum, not a goal. Never apply more than the label directs. Excess nitrogen burns grass and runs off into streams and groundwater.
  • Confusing pounds of product with pounds of nitrogen. 5 lb of 20% fertilizer is 1 lb of nitrogen, not 5.
  • Using the wrong square footage, especially measuring the whole lot.
  • Doubling up on areas where passes overlap, leaving dark green stripes and burned tracks.
  • Applying quick-release at the slow-release rate. Slow-release products tolerate heavier doses; quick-release does not.
  • Treating a larger area than the bag covers, which leaves the lawn underfed.

How many bags should you buy?

Work it through for a real lawn. Say you measured 7,680 sq ft of grass, and you want 1 lb of nitrogen per 1,000 sq ft with a 24-0-6 fertilizer.

  1. Thousands of square feet: 7,680 ÷ 1,000 = 7.68.
  2. Pounds of product per 1,000 sq ft: 100 ÷ 24 = 4.17 lb.
  3. Total product: 7.68 × 4.17 = 32 lb.

A 40 lb bag covers this lawn with 8 lb left over, which keeps for the next feeding. If the bag is sold only in 15 lb sizes, buy three (45 lb) and save the rest. Always buy to the next full bag rather than stretching the product thin.

What does a yearly plan look like in pounds?

For a 5,000 sq ft cool-season lawn that gets 3 lb of nitrogen per 1,000 sq ft over a year in three feedings of 1 lb, using a 24% nitrogen product, you apply about 20.8 lb of product each time, or roughly 62.5 lb across the year. Using a 10% product would need 150 lb. Your state extension service sets the right annual total for your grass; the schedule is in our guide to when to fertilize your lawn.

Frequently asked questions

How many pounds of fertilizer do I need for 5,000 square feet?

With a 20% nitrogen fertilizer at 1 lb of nitrogen per 1,000 sq ft, you need 25 lb of product. With a 30% product, you need about 16.7 lb.

How much fertilizer for 1 acre?

An acre is 43,560 sq ft, so at 1 lb of nitrogen per 1,000 sq ft you need 43.6 lb of nitrogen, which is about 218 lb of a 20% fertilizer.

Is 1 pound of nitrogen per 1,000 square feet too much?

No, it is the standard maximum for quick-release products. Slow-release products can be applied at higher rates per application, but follow the label.

How do I fertilize a small lawn with a hand spreader?

Divide the lawn into 1,000 sq ft sections, measure the amount for one section, and apply that to each. This keeps the rate even on small or irregular yards.

What happens if I put on twice the recommended fertilizer?

You risk leaf burn, brown or yellow streaks, and excessive growth. If it happens, water the lawn deeply to dilute the fertilizer and stay off the area until the grass recovers.

The bottom line

Decide how many pounds of nitrogen your lawn needs (usually 0.5–1 lb per 1,000 sq ft), divide 100 by the bag's nitrogen percentage, and multiply by the number of thousands of square feet.
