Quick answer

Plant onion sets and transplants 2–4 weeks before your last spring frost, as soon as the soil can be worked. Start onion seeds indoors 10–12 weeks before that date. In zones 8–10, plant in fall instead. Choose short-, intermediate- or long-day varieties to match your latitude.

Onions behave differently from most vegetables: they are cold-tolerant, they size up according to day length, and a badly timed start can leave you with thin bulbs or flower stalks. Get the timing and the variety right and onions are one of the easiest crops in the garden.

Key takeaways
  • Spring planting: sets and transplants go in 2–4 weeks before the last frost; onion tops tolerate frost down to roughly 20–25°F once established.
  • Seed: start indoors 10–12 weeks before the last frost (soil germinates at about 50–85°F).
  • Day length matters more than zone: short-day types in the South, long-day types in the North, intermediate in between.
  • Fall planting works in zones 8–10 with short-day varieties, for harvest the following spring.
  • Depth and spacing: 1 inch deep, 4 inches apart; bulbs are ready when about half the tops have fallen over.

When is the best time to plant onions?

The short answer is early. Onions are a cool-season crop that grows roots and leaves in cool weather, then forms a bulb as days lengthen and temperatures rise. Every leaf you grow before bulbing starts becomes one ring of the finished onion, so a big, healthy top in spring means a big bulb in summer. That is why extension services advise getting onions into the ground as soon as the soil can be worked, even 4 weeks before your last frost.

Established onion plants are tougher than they look. Their leaves handle light frosts and freezes into the low 20s°F. What they dislike is a long stretch of cold followed by warmth, which can trigger bolting (more on that below), and ground that is still waterlogged and frozen.

Should you plant onion sets, transplants or seeds?

You can start onions three ways, and each has its own timing and trade-offs.

For a first-time grower, sets are the simplest, and transplants give larger bulbs. If you want a specific variety that suits your day length, growing from seed is the way to get it.

Short-day, intermediate-day or long-day onions: which should you plant?

Onions begin forming bulbs when the daylight reaches a certain number of hours. Plant the wrong type for your latitude and you'll get one of two problems: a short-day onion in the North makes a bulb too early, when the plant is still small, and a long-day onion in the South never gets enough daylight to bulb at all.

TypeBulbs when days reachBest region (approx.)Typical traits
Short-day10–12 hoursSouth of about 35°N (Gulf Coast, Southwest, Florida, southern California)Mild, sweet, shorter storage; plant in fall or winter
Intermediate-day12–14 hoursRoughly 32–42°N (middle band of the country)The most flexible; fine in much of the Midwest, mid-Atlantic and lower Northwest
Long-day14–16 hoursNorth of about 36–40°N (northern states and Canada border)Pungent, store well; plant in spring

The bands overlap, and seed companies label varieties slightly differently, so always follow the day-length description on the packet or catalog. Intermediate-day varieties are the forgiving choice if you live near a boundary, such as Kentucky, Missouri or northern Texas.

Can you plant onions in the fall?

Yes, in the South. In zones 8–10, short-day onions planted in autumn spend winter growing roots and leaves, then bulb in spring as days lengthen. Plant transplants or small sets from about a month before to two weeks after your first fall frost (see the table). The plants should have time to reach about pencil thickness without getting so big that a cold snap triggers flowering.

Further north, fall planting only works for green (bunching) onions or special overwintering types, and results vary. Most gardeners in zones 3–7 will do better with spring planting.

How do you plant onions step by step?

  1. Prepare the bed. Pick a sunny site with loose, well-drained soil that has a pH of about 6.0–7.0. Work in 2 inches of compost. Onions have shallow roots, so keep the top 6 inches loose and weed-free.
  2. Mark the rows. Space rows 12–18 inches apart. In a raised bed, use a grid of 4 inches. A 4 × 8 ft bed (32 sq ft) at 4-inch spacing holds 32 × 9 = 288 plants.
  3. Plant at the right depth. Set sets pointed end up about 1 inch deep, with the tip just showing. Plant transplants 1 inch deep, no deeper, and spread the roots out.
  4. Space them. Plant 4 inches apart for full-size bulbs. If you want green onions too, plant sets 1–2 inches apart and pull every other one as scallions.
  5. Water in well and keep the soil evenly moist, about 1 inch of water per week.
  6. Mulch lightly with 1–2 inches of straw or shredded leaves once plants are established, to hold moisture and suppress weeds.

Starting seed? Sow about 1/4 inch deep in a tray, keep the soil at 65–75°F, and expect sprouts in 7–10 days. Trim the tops to 3–4 inches if they flop over, which encourages stocky seedlings.

Why do onions bolt, and how does planting time affect it?

Bolting means the plant sends up a flower stalk instead of swelling a bulb. A bolted onion can still be eaten, but the center turns hard and it won't store. The most common trigger is a prolonged cool spell (roughly two weeks below 50°F) after the plant has grown beyond pencil thickness, which fools it into thinking winter has passed.

  • Don't plant too early in a cold zone if you are using large transplants or sets bigger than a dime.
  • Pick small sets. Sets about 1/2 inch across (smaller than a quarter) bolt less than big ones.
  • Choose the right day-length variety. The wrong type makes small, early bulbs or no bulbs.
  • Cover plants during a hard freeze that arrives after growth is established.
Mistakes that ruin an onion crop: planting too deep (bulbs will not swell when buried), crowding plants closer than 3 inches, letting weeds compete (onion roots are weak), heavy nitrogen late in the season (it gives thick necks that rot in storage), and picking a long-day variety in the Deep South.

When are onions ready to harvest?

Onions are ready when the tops yellow and about half to three-quarters of them fall over, usually 100–120 days after planting transplants. Stop watering as the tops fall, pull the bulbs on a dry day, and cure them in a warm, shaded, airy spot for 2–3 weeks until the necks are dry. Then store them cool and dry.

Frequently asked questions

Can you plant onion sets in the fall?

In zones 8–10, yes. Plant short-day onion sets or transplants from about a month before to two weeks after the first fall frost and harvest the following spring. In colder zones, fall-planted sets mostly produce green onions.

How deep do you plant onion sets?

Plant sets about 1 inch deep with the pointed end up so the tip is just visible. Planting deeper than 2 inches slows growth and gives smaller, elongated bulbs.

Why do my onions go to flower?

Bolting usually follows a cold spell after the plant has grown larger than a pencil. Use small sets, avoid planting too early in cold zones and pick a day-length variety suited to your latitude.

Is it too late to plant onions in May?

In zones 3–5, May is still on time for sets and transplants. In zones 6 and warmer, onions planted in May often make small bulbs because heat arrives before they have grown enough leaves; use them for green onions instead.

How far apart should you plant onions?

Plant onions 4 inches apart in rows 12–18 inches apart for full-size bulbs. For green onions, 1–2 inches apart is fine.

What soil temperature do onion seeds need to germinate?

Onion seed germinates in soil from about 50°F to 85°F, fastest at 65–75°F. Starting seeds in a warm room indoors is more reliable than sowing into cold spring soil.

The bottom line

Plant onion sets and transplants 2–4 weeks before your last spring frost, start seed indoors 10–12 weeks ahead and, in zones 8–10, plant short-day types in fall.
