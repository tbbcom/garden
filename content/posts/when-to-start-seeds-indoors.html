Quick answer

Start seeds indoors by counting back from your average last frost date: 8 to 10 weeks before for peppers and eggplant, 6 to 8 for tomatoes, 4 to 6 for broccoli, cabbage and lettuce, and 3 to 4 for cucumbers and squash. If your last frost is May 1, that means starting tomatoes around March 13.

The most common seed-starting mistake isn't a bad light or the wrong mix. It's starting too early. Seedlings that stay in pots for 12 weeks get leggy, root-bound and stressed, and they often do worse than younger plants set out on time. Counting back from a reliable date solves it.

This guide gives you the date to count back from, a crop-by-crop weeks chart, a zone-by-zone calendar and tips for hardening off. To find your own frost dates, look them up by ZIP code with the planting calendar.

Key takeaways
  • Count back from your average last spring frost date, not from the calendar month.
  • Peppers and eggplant: 8–10 weeks. Tomatoes: 6–8 weeks. Broccoli, cabbage, lettuce: 4–6 weeks.
  • Squash, cucumbers and melons: only 3–4 weeks; they dislike root disturbance.
  • Carrots, beans, peas, corn and radishes: sow directly outdoors, not indoors.
  • Harden off for 7–10 days before transplanting.

How do you find the right date to start seeds indoors?

You need one number: your average last spring frost date, the day after which a freeze is unlikely. It is an average, so there is still about a 50 percent chance of a frost after it. You can look yours up for free using frost dates by ZIP code, which uses NOAA 1991–2020 climate normals.

  1. Find your last frost date.
  2. Look up the weeks for each crop in the chart below.
  3. Subtract the weeks from your last frost date. That is the day to sow.
  4. Mark the transplant date too, since cool crops go out before the last frost and warm crops after it.

Example: last frost May 5, tomatoes 7 weeks before, so sow on March 17 (May 5 minus 49 days).

What do you need to start seeds indoors?

  • Containers with drainage: cell trays, small pots or recycled containers with holes.
  • Seed-starting mix: a light, sterile, peat- or coir-based mix. Garden soil is too heavy and can carry disease.
  • Light: a south-facing window rarely provides enough. Use a full-spectrum LED or fluorescent shop light 2 to 4 inches above the seedlings, run for 14 to 16 hours a day.
  • Warmth: a heat mat helps peppers, eggplant and tomatoes germinate, which they need at 70 to 85°F.
  • Gentle airflow: a small fan on low strengthens stems and reduces damping-off.

How do you start seeds step by step?

  1. Moisten the mix until it feels like a wrung-out sponge.
  2. Fill cells and sow 2 to 3 seeds per cell at the depth on the packet, usually 2 to 3 times the seed's width.
  3. Cover loosely with a humidity dome or plastic wrap, and keep warm until sprouts appear.
  4. Remove the cover and add light as soon as you see green. Without strong light, seedlings stretch.
  5. Thin to one strong seedling per cell by snipping extras at the soil line.
  6. Water from the bottom and let the surface dry between waterings.
  7. Pot up tomatoes and peppers into 3 to 4 inch pots when they have 2 to 3 sets of true leaves.

How do you harden off seedlings?

Seedlings raised indoors can't take full sun and wind right away. Over 7 to 10 days, give them a gradual outdoor introduction:

  • Days 1–2: 1 to 2 hours in shade, out of the wind.
  • Days 3–4: 3 to 4 hours, with morning sun.
  • Days 5–7: most of the day in sun, bring in at night if it drops below 45°F.
  • Days 8–10: leave out day and night if temperatures stay above 50°F.

Transplant on a cloudy day or in the evening, and water well. Warm-season crops should not go out until nights stay above about 50°F and the soil is at least 60°F.

Don't rush warm-season crops outside. Tomatoes and peppers stall or die in cold soil, and a single night near 32°F kills them. If a late frost is forecast after you've planted, cover plants with row cover, buckets or blankets (not plastic touching the leaves).

What mistakes make seedlings fail?

  1. Starting too early. Old seedlings are leggy and root-bound by transplant day.
  2. Too little light. Tall, pale, floppy seedlings mean they need stronger light placed closer.
  3. Overwatering. Constantly wet mix invites damping-off, a fungus that makes stems collapse at the soil line.
  4. Skipping hardening off. Sunscald and wind burn set plants back by weeks.
  5. Starting direct-sow crops indoors. Carrots, beans, peas and corn resent transplanting and do better sown outside.

Frequently asked questions

Is it too early to start seeds indoors in January?

For most crops, yes. January is right only for slow starters like onions, leeks, and flowers such as petunias, or for gardeners in zones 8 to 10. Tomatoes and peppers started in January are usually leggy by transplant time in colder zones.

Do I need a grow light to start seeds?

Usually yes. Even a bright south window delivers less light than seedlings need, and they stretch. A basic LED shop light 2 to 4 inches above the plants for 14 to 16 hours a day works well.

What is the last frost date and how do I find mine?

It's the average date of the last spring freeze (32°F) in your area. Look it up by ZIP code using NOAA climate normals, and treat it as a guide, not a guarantee.

Which vegetables should be sown directly outdoors?

Carrots, radishes, beets, peas, beans, corn, spinach and turnips grow best from seed sown outside, as they dislike having their roots disturbed.

How long does it take seeds to germinate indoors?

Lettuce and broccoli can sprout in 3 to 7 days, tomatoes in 5 to 10, and peppers in 7 to 14 days at proper temperatures. Check the packet, since old seed or cool conditions can double those times.

Can I start seeds indoors in late spring?

Yes. Heat-loving crops like basil, cucumbers and squash only need 3 to 4 weeks indoors, and in short-summer climates, a late start of fast varieties can still work.

The bottom line

Count back from your average last frost date: 8 to 10 weeks for peppers and eggplant, 6 to 8 for tomatoes, 4 to 6 for cool-season greens and 3 to 4 for squash and cucumbers.
