Quick answer

To protect plants from frost, cover them before sunset when the forecast is 32°F or lower. Use frost cloth, a sheet or buckets, keep covers touching the ground to trap soil heat, and water the soil first. Remove covers in the morning once the temperature rises above freezing.

A frost warning on a spring evening, or an early cold snap in fall, can wipe out a season's tomatoes and peppers in a single night. The good news is that most frost damage is preventable with covers you already own and about ten minutes of work.

This guide covers the temperatures that actually hurt each type of plant, the covers that work (and the ones that don't), what to do on the night, and how to rescue plants that were hit. To see when your area's frost normally arrives, use our frost dates by ZIP code tool.

Key takeaways
  • Frost vs freeze: frost forms at air temperatures near 32°F, while a hard freeze at 28°F or below kills most tender plants.
  • Timing: cover in the late afternoon, before the soil's stored heat escapes.
  • Gain: a typical row cover adds roughly 2–8°F depending on its weight; a bedsheet adds a few degrees.
  • Never: let plastic touch the leaves, or leave covers on in full sun.
  • Hardy crops such as kale, spinach and garlic can handle temperatures well below 32°F.

What temperature is frost, and when does it damage plants?

Frost happens when air near the ground cools to about 32°F (0°C) and moisture freezes on surfaces. Leaves can freeze even when the weather station reports 34–36°F, because clear, still nights cool the ground faster than the air a few feet up.

The National Weather Service uses three terms:

  • Frost advisory: temperatures roughly 33–36°F with clear, calm conditions.
  • Freeze warning: temperatures expected at 32°F or below, during the growing season.
  • Hard freeze: temperatures at or below about 28°F for several hours, which kills most tender plants.

Which plants need frost protection?

Different plants tolerate different lows. Extension services group vegetables by cold hardiness; temperatures below are the approximate lows at which damage begins.

For flowers and landscape plants, new spring growth and open blossoms are the most exposed. Fruit-tree blossoms, for example, can be damaged by temperatures just below 30°F, though the exact point depends on how far the bloom has progressed.

How do you protect plants from frost tonight?

Here is the sequence that works for most home gardens.

Step 1: Check the forecast low and the wind

Frost risk is highest on clear, calm, dry nights. Cloud cover and wind keep air mixed and warmer. If your forecast low is 34°F or colder and the sky is clear, cover.

Step 2: Water the soil in the afternoon

Moist soil holds more heat than dry soil and releases it overnight. Watering earlier in the day lets the water warm up and avoids leaving the foliage wet at night.

Step 3: Cover before sunset

Put covers on by late afternoon so they trap the heat the soil absorbed during the day. Drape fabric over plants, then lay it to the ground and weigh the edges with bricks, boards or soil. Gaps let warm air escape.

Step 4: Support the cover

Stakes, hoops, tomato cages or lawn chairs keep fabric from crushing leaves. The cover should reach to the ground all around.

Step 5: Uncover in the morning

Remove covers once the air is above freezing and the frost on the ground has melted, usually mid-morning. In full sun, covers can overheat plants quickly.

The gains in the table are rough ranges: the real number depends on wind, humidity and how well the cover seals to the ground. Row cover is sold by weight, and heavier fabrics give more protection but pass less light.

Plastic against leaves can do more harm than good. Clear plastic conducts cold, and any leaf that touches it can freeze. If you use plastic, keep it propped away from the foliage and vent it by morning, because it also overheats in sun.

Do string lights, water jugs and mulch really help?

  • Incandescent string lights: old-style bulbs give a small amount of heat under a cover. LED lights give almost none.
  • Water jugs: dark-colored gallon jugs filled with water absorb heat by day and release it at night, which can raise the temperature by a degree or two.
  • Mulch: a layer of straw or leaves protects roots of perennials and garlic. It will not protect leaves above the mulch from frost.
  • Sprinklers: commercial growers use water to protect fruit blossoms because freezing water releases heat. This requires continuous watering all night and can cause ice damage in a home garden, so it is not recommended for most gardeners.

What if frost already hit your plants?

  1. Don't prune right away. Frozen leaves can still shelter the lower parts of the plant. Wait until new growth shows where the damage ends.
  2. Check in a few days. Scratch a stem with a fingernail: green tissue underneath means it is alive.
  3. Water gently if the soil is dry; do not fertilize damaged plants until new leaves appear.
  4. Harvest. Pick green tomatoes and peppers before a hard freeze; green tomatoes will ripen indoors.

Mistakes that undo your frost protection

  1. Covering too late. If the evening air has already dropped, the soil heat has escaped.
  2. Leaving gaps at the ground. Warm air rises out of the cover.
  3. Planting out too early. Tender transplants should wait until after your average last frost date.
  4. Using wet covers. A soaked sheet can freeze solid.
  5. Ignoring pots. Roots in containers are more exposed than in-ground roots. Move pots against a wall or indoors.

How do you protect container plants and young trees?

Pots lose heat faster than in-ground plants because cold air surrounds the roots from every side. Move containers against a south-facing wall, under a porch eave or into a garage or unheated shed for the night. If a pot is too heavy to move, cluster it with others and wrap the pots with burlap or bubble wrap, then cover the plants themselves with fabric.

Young fruit trees and tender shrubs can be wrapped from the ground up with frost cloth, tied loosely at the top. Remove the wrap in the morning so the plant is not overheated by midday sun. For blossoms on fruit trees, cover on the nights when the forecast is below about 30°F, since open flowers are more sensitive than dormant buds.

Frequently asked questions

Will a frost kill my tomato plants?

A light frost at 32°F can blacken tomato leaves and kill young plants. Tomatoes are damaged by temperatures near freezing and are also stressed when nights fall below about 50°F, so cover them or wait until after the last frost to plant.

Is it too cold to leave plants uncovered at 40°F?

Usually not for the plants themselves, but clear, calm nights can let leaf temperatures drop below the forecast. If the forecast is 36°F or lower with no wind or clouds, cover tender plants.

Can I use a plastic tarp to cover plants?

You can for a night or two if it is held off the foliage on stakes or hoops and removed in the morning. Fabric is safer because it breathes and does not conduct cold to the leaves.

Do covered plants need watering before a freeze?

Yes. Moist soil stores and releases more heat than dry soil, so water in the afternoon before a frost night.

How long can I leave row cover on?

Light and medium row covers can stay on for days or weeks during cold weather because they let light and rain through. Remove them to allow pollination of crops such as squash, and vent them on sunny days.

The bottom line

Know your plants' limits, cover them before sunset with fabric that reaches the ground, and uncover them in the morning. Plan around your average frost dates and use the garden calculators to plan a layout you can cover easily.
