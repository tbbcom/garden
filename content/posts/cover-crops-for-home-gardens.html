Quick answer

The best cover crop for most home gardens is winter rye, sown 4–6 weeks before your first hard freeze at roughly 2–3 lb per 1,000 sq ft. Use crimson clover or hairy vetch to add nitrogen, and oats where you want a crop that winterkills. Cut or mow cover crops before they set seed, and wait 2–3 weeks before planting.

Cover crops are plants grown for the soil rather than for the table. Sown in fall on a bare bed, they hold soil in place, crowd out weeds, feed worms and soil microbes and add organic matter when you cut them in spring. They are among the cheapest ways to improve a vegetable garden, with seed costing a few dollars for a typical bed.

This guide helps you choose a cover crop, work out how much seed you need and know when to sow and end it. If you're planning the soil volume for new beds at the same time, the soil calculator makes quick work of that.

Key takeaways
  • Winter rye: the toughest; about 2–3 lb per 1,000 sq ft (3–4 oz per 100 sq ft), sown until about 4 weeks before the ground freezes.
  • Legumes (crimson clover, hairy vetch, Austrian winter pea) add nitrogen but need earlier sowing.
  • Oats grow fast in fall and usually winterkill in cold zones, leaving easy-to-plant mulch.
  • End the crop before it goes to seed, then wait 2–3 weeks before planting.
  • Seeding rates on the packet may differ; always follow the label.

What is a cover crop and why use one?

A cover crop is a plant sown to protect and improve soil between food crops. Benefits that extension and USDA-NRCS sources describe include:

  • Erosion control: roots and leaves keep rain from carrying soil away.
  • Weed suppression: dense growth crowds out winter and early-spring weeds.
  • Organic matter: the cut crop breaks down into humus that improves structure and water-holding.
  • Nitrogen from legumes: clovers, vetch and peas host bacteria that capture nitrogen from the air; some is released as the plants decompose.
  • Nutrient capture: grasses such as rye hold leftover nutrients that would otherwise leach out.

How do you sow a cover crop?

Step 1: Clear the bed

Remove spent plants and weeds. A cover crop will not outcompete large weeds.

Step 2: Loosen the surface

Rake the top inch or two. No deep digging is needed.

Step 3: Broadcast the seed evenly

Mix small seed with a handful of sand or dry soil to spread it evenly. Walk in two directions so you cover the whole bed.

Step 4: Rake and press

Rake the seed lightly into the soil to a depth of about ½ to 1 inch for large seed like rye and peas, shallower for clover. Tamp or walk over the soil to improve contact.

Step 5: Water and wait

Keep the soil moist until seedlings show, usually within 7–14 days. Legume seed can be dusted with the matching inoculant (sold with the seed) to help nitrogen-fixing bacteria colonize the roots.

When and how do you end a cover crop in spring?

Timing matters more than method. Cut, mow or flatten the crop before it sets seed (for rye, before it heads out; for clover and vetch, at early bloom). If left to seed, rye and vetch can become weeds.

  • Cut and leave on the surface: use a string trimmer, mower or shears, then leave the residue as mulch and plant through it.
  • Cut and cover: lay a tarp or cardboard for 3–6 weeks to smother regrowth, especially for rye.
  • Turn under: if you do dig, incorporate the green material at least 2–3 weeks before planting. Fresh residue ties up nitrogen as it breaks down, and rye can inhibit small-seeded crops like carrots and lettuce for a few weeks.
  • Winterkilled crops such as oats can be raked back and planted into directly.
Rye can be hard to manage in a small bed. If your garden is mostly hand tools and you aren't comfortable cutting and smothering a dense mat, choose oats (winterkills in cold zones) or crimson clover instead of winter rye.

Can you use cover crops in raised beds?

Yes. Raised beds suit cover crops because they are small and easy to cut by hand. Choose oats or crimson clover if you want to plant early. After cutting, add 1–2 inches of compost on top before planting. See our fall routine for winterizing raised beds with the right soil volume.

Common cover crop mistakes

  1. Sowing too late. Seed needs time to establish; late sowing gives thin cover.
  2. Letting it go to seed. Rye and vetch become weeds if allowed to mature.
  3. Planting right after turning under. Wait two to three weeks to avoid nitrogen tie-up and allelopathy.
  4. Using cover crops with the same family as your vegetables. Peas and vetch share some diseases with garden peas and beans.
  5. Skipping the seed rate. Too little leaves gaps for weeds; too much wastes seed.

What cover crops work in summer?

If a bed will sit empty for six weeks or more in summer, a warm-season cover crop keeps weeds down and builds soil. Buckwheat is the usual choice: it sprouts within days, flowers in about four to six weeks, feeds bees and is killed by the first frost. Cut it at the first flowers and before seeds form. Cowpeas and sunn hemp fix nitrogen and suit the South, where summers are long.

Turn the cut residue into a mulch layer or work it lightly into the top inch of soil, then plant a fall crop after a week or two.

Frequently asked questions

What is the easiest cover crop for a beginner?

Oats are the easiest: they germinate quickly, grow fast in fall and winterkill in cold zones, leaving a mulch you can plant through. Winter rye is more effective but harder to remove.

How late can I plant a cover crop in fall?

Winter rye can go in up to about four weeks before the ground freezes. Legumes and oats need six to ten weeks of mild weather to establish.

Do cover crops attract pests?

They can shelter slugs and voles, but they also host beneficial insects. Cutting them before the main crop and keeping mulch back from stems reduces pest problems.

How long before planting vegetables after a cover crop?

Wait two to three weeks after cutting or turning under a cover crop. For rye, waiting three to four weeks helps seed germination in direct-sown crops.

Can I grow a cover crop in summer?

Yes. Buckwheat and cowpeas grow fast in warm weather and can fill a bed that would otherwise sit empty for 6–8 weeks.

The bottom line

Pick winter rye for toughness, oats for ease or crimson clover for nitrogen, sow it at the right rate for your bed and end it before it sets seed. Match your sowing date to your first frost, and plan the spring soil refresh with the soil calculator.
