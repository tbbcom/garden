Quick answer

A 4 × 8 ft raised bed needs about 20 gallons of water a week for 1 inch of watering. With three drip lines and a 0.6 gph emitter every 12 inches, that takes about 83 minutes a week, split into 2–3 sessions. Connect a timer, filter and pressure regulator at the spigot, then run tubing along the bed.

Hand-watering raised beds works until you go on vacation or a heat wave hits. Drip irrigation puts the water at the roots, a little at a time, on a schedule. It wastes less water, keeps leaves dry (which helps with fungal disease) and takes about an afternoon to install.

This guide shows the parts you need, a simple layout for a 4 × 8 bed, a run-time chart by emitter size and the mistakes that cause clogs and dry spots. For other bed sizes, the irrigation runtime calculator works out minutes from your flow rate.

Key takeaways
  • Water need: 1 inch of water over 1 sq ft is 0.623 gallons, so a 4 × 8 bed (32 sq ft) needs 19.9 gallons per inch.
  • Layout: three lines per 4 ft bed, with emitters every 12 inches.
  • Run time: about 83 minutes per week with 0.6 gph emitters, 50 minutes with 1 gph emitters.
  • Parts at the spigot: timer, filter, pressure regulator, then tubing.
  • Adjust: add water in heat, with sandy soil or for seedlings, and cut back after rain.

Most vegetable gardens need about 1 inch of water per week from rain and irrigation combined, which is the figure most US extension services give, more in hot, windy weather or sandy soil. One inch of water on one square foot is 0.623 gallons, so:

What parts do you need for drip irrigation in a raised bed?

A basic kit has these parts, connected in this order from the hose bib.

  1. Timer: a battery hose-end timer is enough for one zone.
  2. Backflow preventer: required in many areas; ask your local water utility or check the kit.
  3. Filter: a 150–200 mesh filter stops grit that blocks emitters.
  4. Pressure regulator: drip lines are designed for low pressure, commonly around 25 psi.
  5. Mainline tubing: 1/2 inch poly tubing from the spigot to the beds.
  6. Drip lines or emitter tubing: 1/4 inch lines with inline emitters, or 1/2 inch tubing with built-in emitters spaced 12 inches apart.
  7. Connectors, stakes and end caps: to hold lines in place and close the ends.

How do you set up drip irrigation in a raised bed?

Step 1: Plan the lines

In a 4 ft wide bed, run three lines along the length, spaced about 16 inches apart and 8 inches from each side. For a 4 × 8 bed that's three 8 ft lines, or one long line looped back and forth. Emitters every 12 inches give each plant a water source within a few inches.

Step 2: Connect the spigot assembly

Thread the timer onto the spigot, then the backflow preventer if needed, then the filter, then the pressure regulator. Attach the 1/2 inch mainline to the regulator outlet.

Step 3: Run the mainline to the bed

Lay the tubing in the sun for a few minutes so it softens and uncoils. Run it to the bed edge and add a tee or a manifold for each bed.

Step 4: Lay out and stake the drip lines

Connect each drip line to the mainline with a fitting and stake it every 2–3 feet. Close the far end with an end cap or a figure-8 end clamp.

Step 5: Flush, test and check coverage

Open the ends briefly to flush dirt, then close them and run the system. Walk the bed: every emitter should drip steadily, with no leaks at connections. Dig a small hole after a run to see how far the water spreads sideways and down; this is your real coverage.

How often should you water raised beds with drip?

Split the weekly total into two or three sessions so water soaks deeper and plants don't sit wet. Raised bed soil drains faster than ground soil, so sandy mixes may need shorter, more frequent runs.

  • Seedlings and new transplants: short runs every day or two until roots establish, then switch to longer, less frequent runs.
  • Established vegetables in warm weather: 2–3 deeper sessions per week.
  • Heat waves: raise the total toward 1.5 inches, spread over more days.
  • After heavy rain: skip a session. A rain gauge or a timer with a rain sensor saves water.

Check with a finger or trowel. If the top 2 inches are dry, water; if soggy, wait. Mulching the bed with straw or shredded leaves slows evaporation.

How many beds can one spigot run?

A 4 × 8 bed with 24 emitters at 0.6 gph uses only 14.4 gallons per hour, or 0.24 gallons per minute. A typical hose spigot delivers several gallons per minute, so one spigot can feed many beds. Eight such beds use about 1.9 gpm. Still, check the maximum tubing run length in your kit's instructions, since long runs of 1/2 inch tubing lose pressure and the far beds get less.

Don't skip the filter and regulator. Drip emitters have tiny openings that clog with grit, and full house pressure can pop fittings and split tubing. Both parts are cheap and are the two most common reasons beginner systems fail.

What are the most common drip irrigation mistakes?

  1. Lines too far from the plants. Emitters should be within a few inches of each root zone; in sandy soil, water spreads down more than sideways.
  2. Running too long, too rarely. Long, infrequent runs can waste water below the roots in sandy soil.
  3. Burying lines under mulch and forgetting them. Check emitters monthly for clogs and leaks.
  4. Mixing flow rates on one line. Emitters of different sizes make dry and wet patches. Use one flow rate per zone.
  5. Leaving it on in winter. Drain or disconnect the system before freezing weather, since water in tubing and fittings can crack them.

Frequently asked questions

How many drip lines do I need for a 4-foot-wide raised bed?

Three lines spaced about 16 inches apart covers most vegetable beds evenly. A narrower 2–3 ft bed can use two lines.

Is drip irrigation better than a sprinkler for a vegetable garden?

Usually yes. Drip puts water at the roots, wastes less to evaporation and keeps foliage dry, which helps reduce leaf diseases. Sprinklers still suit lawns and seed beds that need an even surface mist.

What psi should a drip irrigation system run at?

Most drip systems are designed for low pressure, commonly around 10–30 psi, and a regulator set near 25 psi is typical. Check your tubing and emitter specs for the exact range.

Can I use a soaker hose instead of drip tubing in a raised bed?

Yes, soaker hoses work in a small bed and are simpler to install. Their output is less even over long runs and they clog and wear sooner than emitter-based drip tubing.

How do I know if my drip system is watering deep enough?

After a run, dig a small hole near an emitter. The soil should be moist 6–8 inches down for most vegetables. If it's dry below 3 inches, increase the run time; if it's wet beyond the root zone, shorten it.
