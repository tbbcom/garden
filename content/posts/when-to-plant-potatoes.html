Quick answer

Plant potatoes 2–4 weeks before your average last spring frost, as soon as the soil can be worked and has warmed to 45–50°F. That means March in the South, mid-April in zones 5–6 and late April to mid-May in zones 3–4. Seed pieces tolerate cool soil but not wet, frozen ground.

Potatoes are one of the few vegetables you should plant before the last frost. They are a cool-season crop: tubers form best when soil is 60–70°F, and heat above about 80°F slows them badly. Planting early lets the crop bulk up before summer.

Below you will find potato-specific dates for USDA zones 3–10, the soil temperature to look for, and the planting depth and hilling steps. Look up your frost dates by ZIP code, then use the planting calendar to turn them into a date for your garden.

Key takeaways
  • Plant date: 2–4 weeks before the average last frost.
  • Soil temperature: at least 45°F (many extension guides say 40°F minimum) and not waterlogged.
  • Seed pieces: use certified seed potatoes, cut into 1.5–2 oz chunks with 1–2 eyes each.
  • Depth and spacing: 3–4 inches deep, 10–12 inches apart, rows 30–36 inches apart.
  • Harvest: new potatoes at about 60–70 days, mature potatoes at 90–120 days.

When is the best time to plant potatoes?

The standard recommendation from extension services is to plant two to four weeks before the average last frost, when soil has dried enough to dig without clumping and has reached 45–50°F. Seed pieces sit quietly in cool soil for two to four weeks while sprouts develop, and shoots usually emerge after the hardest freezes are past.

Potato foliage is frost-tender. If shoots emerge and a frost is forecast, cover them with loose soil or row cover. Plants usually regrow from a light frost, though growth is delayed.

Take a morning reading 4 inches deep. If the soil is soggy, wait. A handful that crumbles when squeezed is ready; a handful that stays in a ball is too wet.

How do you prepare the soil before planting potatoes?

Potatoes want loose, well-drained soil so tubers can swell without deforming. Work the bed 8–10 inches deep as soon as it is workable, and mix in 2 inches of finished compost. Heavy clay benefits from extra organic matter or a raised bed; soggy ground is the most common cause of rot.

Keep soil pH in the range of 5.0–6.5. Higher pH favors common scab, which makes rough, corky patches on the skin. Do not lime the bed just before planting unless a soil test says you need it, and avoid fresh manure for the same reason. A soil test through your county extension office costs little and tells you exactly what to add.

Potatoes are also heavy feeders. Many extension guides suggest a balanced fertilizer worked in at planting, then a light side-dressing when plants are about 6 inches tall. Follow the product label for rates, and keep fertilizer from touching the seed pieces directly.

Can you plant potatoes in fall?

In zones 9–10 a fall crop is possible. Plant in late summer so tubers form as temperatures drop below 80°F, and harvest before a hard freeze or into winter, depending on the area. Check local extension recommendations for exact dates, because summer heat can rot seed pieces. In colder zones, potatoes are a spring-planted crop only.

What are seed potatoes, and can you plant grocery store potatoes?

Seed potatoes are tubers certified to be free of common diseases. Garden centers sell them in spring, usually as whole small potatoes or larger ones to cut. Grocery store potatoes are risky: they are often treated to stop sprouting and can carry diseases such as late blight or viruses. Buy certified seed for the best results.

Potatoes come in early (about 70–90 days), mid-season (about 90–110 days) and late (about 110–135 days) types. Early varieties suit short-season zones 3–4 and hot-summer areas. Late varieties yield more where seasons are long and store longest.

How do you plant potatoes step by step?

  1. Chit the seed (optional). Set seed potatoes in a bright, cool (60°F) spot for 2–3 weeks until stubby sprouts form.
  2. Cut large tubers into pieces of 1.5–2 oz (about egg-sized) with at least one or two eyes. Let cut pieces cure for 1–2 days so the surface dries.
  3. Prepare the bed. Choose full sun and loose, well-drained soil with a pH near 5.0–6.5. Avoid fresh manure, which encourages scab.
  4. Dig a trench 4 inches deep. Place pieces cut side down, eyes up, 10–12 inches apart, in rows 30–36 inches apart.
  5. Cover with 3–4 inches of soil. Water in once, then hold off until shoots appear unless the soil is dry.
  6. Hill the plants. When shoots are 6–8 inches tall, mound soil around them, leaving the top few leaves exposed. Repeat once or twice.

A 4 × 8 ft raised bed fits 2 rows of 8 plants (rows 2 ft apart, plants 12 inches apart), or 16 plants; denser raised-bed spacing of about 9 inches fits roughly 20. Try the plant spacing calculator to plan your bed.

Why does hilling matter for potatoes?

Tubers form above the seed piece, and any exposed to light turn green. That green skin contains solanine, which tastes bitter and can make you sick, so cut away green parts and never eat them. Hilling covers the new tubers with dark soil, supports the plants and encourages more yield. Straw mulch piled 6–8 inches deep works as an alternative to soil hills.

Which potato planting mistakes should you avoid?

Do not plant into wet, cold soil. Cut seed pieces rot quickly when soil is saturated and cold. If you cannot squeeze a handful of soil apart easily, wait a few days.
  1. Planting whole or cut pieces from the grocery store. Disease risk is much higher than with certified seed.
  2. Cutting seed too small. Pieces under 1 ounce lack the energy for strong sprouts.
  3. Planting too late. Tubers stop forming when soil stays above 80°F, so late plantings in hot zones give small yields.
  4. Skipping hills. Exposed tubers turn green and bitter.
  5. Overwatering after planting. Wait until sprouts emerge unless the soil is very dry.
  6. Repeating the crop in the same bed. Rotate away from potatoes, tomatoes, peppers and eggplant for about three years.

Frequently asked questions

Can you plant potatoes too early?

Yes, if the soil is frozen or waterlogged. Seed pieces tolerate cool soil at 40–45°F for a few weeks, but cold, wet ground can cause rot. Wait until the soil can be worked and crumbles in your hand.

Do potatoes need to be planted on Good Friday?

No. Good Friday is a folk tradition and has no scientific basis. Soil temperature and your last frost date are the reliable signals.

How long do potatoes take to grow?

Early varieties give small new potatoes in about 60–70 days and full size in 70–90 days. Late varieties take 110–135 days. Mature potatoes are ready when the foliage yellows and dies back.

Should I let cut seed potatoes dry before planting?

Yes. Leave cut pieces in a cool, humid spot for 1–2 days so the cut surface heals. This helps prevent rot in the soil.

How deep should seed potatoes be planted?

Plant seed pieces 3–4 inches deep, then hill soil around the growing stems as plants reach 6–8 inches tall.

The bottom line

Plant potatoes 2–4 weeks before your average last frost, when the soil is 45–50°F and crumbly rather than soggy. Use certified seed, plant 3–4 inches deep and hill as the plants grow. Use the planting calendar to turn your ZIP code into a planting window.
