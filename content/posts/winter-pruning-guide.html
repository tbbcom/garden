Quick answer

Prune in winter when plants are dormant, but only those that bloom on new growth or need structural cuts. Late winter is best for apple, pear, grape, roses, butterfly bush and most shade trees. Wait until after bloom for lilac, forsythia and azalea, and never remove more than a quarter of a tree's live crown.

Dormant pruning is one of the best garden jobs for a cold, clear day. Without leaves you can see the structure of a tree or shrub, and the cuts heal quickly when growth restarts in spring. But pruning at the wrong time costs you a year of flowers or fruit, so it pays to know which plants belong on the winter list.

This guide gives you a plant-by-plant timing table, the cuts to make, tools to use and the mistakes to avoid. If your cuts leave wood to mulch or compost, the mulch calculator helps you plan what to do with the clippings.

Key takeaways
  • Best window: late winter, a few weeks before buds swell, for most dormant pruning.
  • Prune in winter: apple, pear, grape, blueberry, shade trees, roses, butterfly bush, panicle hydrangea.
  • Wait until after bloom: lilac, forsythia, azalea, rhododendron, bigleaf hydrangea, spring-blooming spirea.
  • Limit: remove no more than about 25% of a tree's live canopy in one year.
  • Cut point: about ¼ inch above a bud, never flush with the trunk or leaving long stubs.

Is winter a good time to prune?

For many plants, yes. Dormant plants are not using energy for leaves and flowers, so pruning wounds are less stressful, insects and many disease spores are inactive, and bare branches reveal crossing or dead limbs. Late winter, just before growth starts, is also when a pruning cut closes fastest.

The exceptions matter. Plants that bloom on last year's wood already carry next summer's flower buds in winter; pruning them now removes the flowers. In very cold regions, pruning stone fruit or tender shrubs in mid-winter can lead to dieback. And a few trees are best pruned at other times for disease reasons, such as oaks (avoid April through July, when beetles spreading oak wilt are active).

How do you make a proper pruning cut?

Step 1: Start with the three Ds

Remove dead, damaged and diseased wood first. These cuts help the plant at any time of year and often make the rest of the job obvious.

Step 2: Remove crossing and rubbing branches

Where two branches rub, wounds form and invite disease. Keep the stronger or better-placed one.

Step 3: Cut just above a bud

When shortening a branch, cut at a slight angle about ¼ inch above a healthy bud that points the direction you want growth to go, usually outward.

Step 4: Cut large limbs in three steps

For a limb thicker than 2 inches, make an undercut about 12 inches out, then cut from the top a few inches beyond it so the limb falls without tearing the bark. Finally, remove the stub just outside the branch collar, the swollen ring where the branch joins the trunk. Do not cut flush with the trunk, and do not leave a long stub.

Step 5: Skip wound paint

Current extension guidance does not recommend wound dressing for most pruning cuts; trees seal wounds themselves, and a clean cut is better than a coating.

How much can you prune at once?

The International Society of Arboriculture advises that no more than about one quarter of a tree's live crown should be removed in a single season; for mature trees, a lower amount is safer. Removing more triggers a flush of weak, fast-growing shoots called water sprouts.

Shrubs differ. Some tolerate rejuvenation pruning: cutting a third of the oldest stems to the ground each year for three years renews dogwoods, spirea and many other multi-stemmed shrubs without losing all their flowers. Hard cuts to 6–12 inches suit butterfly bush and similar plants that bloom on new growth.

Use bypass tools (blades that slide past each other) rather than anvil tools, which crush stems. Sharp blades make clean cuts that heal faster. If you are cutting out diseased wood, wipe the blades with 70% rubbing alcohol between cuts so you do not spread the problem.

Stay away from power lines and hire a pro for big limbs. Trees with branches near utility lines or limbs thicker than about 4–6 inches overhead are a job for a certified arborist. Falling limbs injure people every year.

6 winter pruning mistakes to avoid

  1. Pruning spring bloomers in winter. You'll cut off this year's flowers.
  2. Topping trees. Cutting main branches to stubs weakens the tree and leads to weak regrowth.
  3. "Volcano" pruning of crape myrtle. Cutting to thick knuckles every year is unnecessary; remove only crossing limbs and small twigs.
  4. Pruning in a deep freeze. Wood is brittle and cuts can split; wait for a mild day.
  5. Leaving stubs or cutting flush. Both slow healing; cut just outside the branch collar.
  6. Using dull or dirty tools. They tear bark and spread disease.

How do you prune an apple tree in winter?

Apple and pear trees are the classic dormant-pruning job. Aim for an open center that lets light reach every branch, because fruit ripens and colors best in sunlight.

  1. Remove dead, broken and diseased wood.
  2. Cut out water sprouts, the fast vertical shoots that grow from branches, and suckers at the base.
  3. Thin one of any two branches that cross or rub.
  4. Shorten branches that hang low enough to touch the ground.
  5. Step back often. You can always take more off next year, but you can't put a branch back.

Young trees need training more than pruning, so remove only what is necessary during the first few years and let the tree build a strong frame.

Frequently asked questions

What month is best for winter pruning?

February to March is best for most dormant pruning, a few weeks before buds swell. In warm climates, January works; in cold climates, wait until the hardest freezes are over.

Can I prune trees when it is below freezing?

You can, but wood is brittle in deep cold and tender plants may suffer dieback. Choose a mild day when temperatures are above about 20°F if you must prune in the cold.

Which shrubs should never be pruned in winter?

Spring-flowering shrubs such as lilac, forsythia, azalea, rhododendron and bigleaf hydrangea have their flower buds formed by winter, so wait until after they bloom.

Should I seal pruning cuts with paint?

No. Extension services generally advise against wound dressings, because trees compartmentalize wounds on their own and the coating can trap moisture.

Is it too late to prune once buds break?

For many trees and shrubs, light pruning after buds open is fine, but heavy pruning at that stage wastes stored energy. Fruit trees do best if pruned before bud break.

Can I prune roses in winter?

Do major pruning of hybrid tea and floribunda roses in late winter to early spring as buds swell. In fall and early winter, trim only long canes that could whip in the wind.

The bottom line

Prune dormant fruit trees, shade trees, grapes, roses and summer-blooming shrubs in late winter, and leave spring bloomers until after they flower. Remove the three Ds first, take no more than a quarter of the crown and make clean cuts. Chip the prunings and use the mulch calculator to plan where they go.
