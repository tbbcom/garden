Quick answer

In square foot gardening, each 12 × 12 in square holds 1, 4, 9 or 16 plants, depending on the crop's mature spread. Large plants such as cabbage or broccoli get one square, leaf lettuce and chard get 4, bush beans and spinach get 9, and carrots and radishes get 16. To space any crop, divide 12 by its spacing in inches and square the result.

Square foot gardening (SFG) turns a raised bed into a grid of one-foot squares and plants each square at a fixed density. The appeal is simple: no row math, no wasted paths, and a plan you can sketch on the back of an envelope. The catch is that the "right" number of plants per square depends on the crop, and most charts only cover a handful of vegetables.

This spacing chart covers the common crops, shows the formula so you can work out any vegetable that isn't listed, and includes a ready-made plan for a 4 × 8 bed. If you'd rather skip the arithmetic, the plant spacing calculator gives plant counts for any bed size and crop.

Key takeaways
  • Four densities: 1, 4, 9 or 16 plants per square foot, based on 12 in, 6 in, 4 in or 3 in spacing.
  • Formula: (12 ÷ spacing in inches) squared = plants per square foot, rounded down.
  • Big plants take more than one square: peppers and broccoli at 18 in need about 4 squares each in standard extension spacing.
  • A 4 × 8 bed has 32 squares, enough for a mixed plan of roughly 8 to 12 different crops.
  • Grids need a trellis plan: put tall or climbing crops on the north side so they don't shade shorter ones.

How do you calculate plants per square foot?

Every square foot is 12 inches by 12 inches. If a crop wants plants spaced s inches apart in every direction, you can fit 12 ÷ s plants along each side of the square. Multiply that number by itself and you have the plants per square, rounded down because you can't plant half a seedling.

  1. Find the recommended spacing on the seed packet or plant tag (use the in-row spacing for the thinned plants).
  2. Divide 12 by that spacing. For 4 in spacing: 12 ÷ 4 = 3.
  3. Square the result. 3 × 3 = 9 plants per square foot.
  4. If the spacing is larger than 12 in, work out how many squares one plant needs: (spacing ÷ 12) squared, rounded up. For 18 in: 1.5 squared = 2.25, so allow 4 squares (a 2 × 2 block) or one plant per 2.25 sq ft if you're mixing neighbors.

The method comes from Mel Bartholomew's book Square Foot Gardening. His original chart uses the same four densities. Extension spacing for some crops (tomatoes and peppers especially) is wider than the one-plant-per-square shortcut, so the chart below follows mainstream extension spacing and tells you when a plant needs more room.

How do you space big plants like tomatoes and peppers in a grid?

Here the SFG shortcut and extension advice part ways. A single square gives a pepper plant only 12 inches in each direction, while extension services typically recommend 18 inches between pepper plants and 18 to 36 inches between tomatoes depending on how they're trained. Crowded plants have less airflow, more disease and smaller harvests.

The practical fix is to give big plants a 2 × 2 block of four squares. Plant the tomato or pepper in the middle of the block and use the corners for something low and quick, such as radishes, basil or lettuce, that will be harvested before the main plant fills in. In a 4 × 8 bed, this fits 8 staked tomatoes at 24 in if the whole bed is given over to them, but a mixed bed is usually better.

Mind the shade. Tomatoes on a 6-foot stake or trellis throw shade over their neighbors. Put them on the north side of the bed (the south side if you're in the Southern Hemisphere) so sun-loving crops to the south keep getting light.

How do you set up a square foot garden grid?

  1. Build or choose a bed no wider than 4 feet, so you can reach the middle from either side without stepping on the soil.
  2. Fill it with loose, rich soil. Square foot gardening depends on good soil because plants are close together. Work out how much you need with the soil calculator.
  3. Mark the grid permanently. Wood lath, thin strips of lumber or heavy string stapled to the bed edge all work. Marked squares prevent the slow drift to uneven spacing.
  4. Plant by square, not by row. Press seeds to the right depth (usually 2 to 3 times the seed's width), then thin to the target number once seedlings have two sets of true leaves.
  5. Water and feed more often than a row garden. Densely planted squares use water and nutrients faster. Top up with 1 inch of compost between crops.

When should you replant a square?

One of the best features of the grid is succession planting. When you pull a spent crop, the square is ready for something new in minutes. Loosen the top 2 inches, rake in a handful of compost, and replant.

  • Quick crops (30 to 45 days): radishes, baby lettuce and spinach are ready in 3 to 6 weeks, so a single square can produce several harvests in a season.
  • Season-long crops: tomatoes, peppers and kale hold their squares until frost.
  • Cool-season swaps: after summer beans finish, sow carrots, beets or fall lettuce in the same squares. Check your timing with the planting calendar.

What mistakes make square foot gardens fail?

  1. Treating every plant as a one-square crop. Peppers, broccoli and tomatoes need a full block in standard extension spacing.
  2. Overcrowding without thinning. Sowing 16 carrot seeds is fine; leaving 16 untouched when 10 germinate is not. Thin to the target number early.
  3. Shading the south side. Tall crops belong on the north edge.
  4. Skimping on soil. Dense planting needs fertile, loose soil at least 6 to 12 inches deep.
  5. Forgetting airflow. In humid climates, give tomatoes and squash extra room so leaves dry quickly after rain.

Frequently asked questions

How many carrots can you plant in one square foot?

Sixteen carrot seeds fit in a square foot at 3 inch spacing. Sow a few extra and thin to 16 once seedlings are 2 inches tall.

Can you grow tomatoes in square foot gardening?

Yes, but give each plant at least a 2 × 2 foot block of four squares, which equals 24 inch spacing, and grow it up a stake or cage. One plant in a single square is too crowded for most varieties.

How many squares does a 4×8 raised bed have?

A 4 × 8 foot bed has 32 squares, because 4 × 8 = 32 square feet. A 4 × 4 bed has 16 squares and a 3 × 6 bed has 18.

Is square foot gardening worth it?

It works best for small spaces and beginners because the grid makes spacing simple and keeps beds compact and easy to weed. The trade-off is that dense planting needs richer soil and more frequent watering than wide rows.

How do you thin seedlings in a square foot garden?

Snip the extra seedlings at soil level with scissors, rather than pulling them, so you don't disturb the roots of the plants you're keeping. Thin when seedlings have two sets of true leaves and keep the strongest ones.

What is the best spacing for lettuce in a square?

Four leaf lettuce plants per square (6 inch spacing) is standard. Heading types like romaine and butterhead do best at one plant per square if you want full-size heads.

The bottom line

For any crop, divide 12 by its spacing in inches and square the answer: that is how many plants fit in a square foot. Most vegetables land on 1, 4, 9 or 16, and big plants like tomatoes and peppers need a 2 × 2 block of squares.
