Quick answer

USDA hardiness zones divide North America into 13 zones based on the average coldest winter temperature, each 10°F wide and split into 5°F "a" and "b" halves. Zone 6 means winter lows of −10°F to 0°F. A plant "hardy to zone 5" should survive a typical winter low of −20°F to −10°F.

If you've ever read a plant tag that said "Zones 4–8," you've met the USDA Plant Hardiness Zone Map. It's the standard US reference for choosing trees, shrubs and perennials that will survive your winters. It's also one of the most misunderstood numbers in gardening, because many people assume it predicts frost dates, summer heat or growing season length. It doesn't.

This guide explains how the zones work, how to find yours, how to read plant tags and what the map leaves out. For when to actually plant, pair your zone with the dates in the planting calendar.

Key takeaways
  • Zones are based on one thing: the average annual lowest winter temperature (1991–2020 data in the 2023 map).
  • Each zone is 10°F wide; each half zone ("a" or "b") is 5°F.
  • Lower numbers mean colder winters. Zone 3 gets to −40°F to −30°F; zone 9 only 20°F to 30°F.
  • Zones don't predict frost dates, heat, rainfall or snow cover.
  • Pick plants rated for your zone or a lower number (a zone 4 plant is fine in zone 6).

What are USDA hardiness zones?

The USDA Plant Hardiness Zone Map, produced by the USDA Agricultural Research Service with Oregon State University's PRISM Climate Group, divides the US into zones by the average annual extreme minimum winter temperature, the single coldest night of each winter averaged over 30 years. Each zone is a 10°F band, and each band is split into two 5°F half zones, labeled "a" for the colder half and "b" for the warmer half.

The current map was released in November 2023 and uses weather data from 1991 to 2020. The USDA reports that about half of the country moved into a warmer half zone compared with the 2012 map, and the other half stayed the same. If your zone changed, your plants didn't, but the average lowest temperatures in your area did.

How do I find my hardiness zone?

  1. Use the official USDA map. The USDA Plant Hardiness Zone Map website lets you enter a ZIP code and shows your zone and temperature range.
  2. Check the half zone, not just the number. Zone 7a and 7b can differ in what survives.
  3. Note your frost dates separately using frost dates by ZIP code, because your zone doesn't give them.

How do you read a plant tag with zone numbers?

A tag that says "Zones 4–9" tells you the plant's hardy range. The first number is the coldest winter it can survive on average. The second is usually the hottest area where it still does well, or the warm limit before it struggles from lack of winter chill or from heat.

  • Your zone should fall inside the range. If you're in zone 6, a plant labeled 4–8 is a good match.
  • Choose margin for borderline plants. If you're at the cold edge of the range, plant in a sheltered spot and mulch heavily.
  • Container plants are less hardy. Roots in pots are exposed to cold from all sides. A common extension rule of thumb is to treat a pot as one to two zones colder than the ground.
  • Annual vegetables don't use zones. Tomatoes and beans are killed by frost in every zone, so frost dates matter, not hardiness.

What do hardiness zones not tell you?

Zones are a single measurement, which means several things that affect plants are missing:

  • Frost dates and growing season length. Two places in the same zone can have last-frost dates a month apart.
  • Summer heat. The American Horticultural Society publishes a separate Heat Zone Map (zones 1–12), based on the number of days per year above 86°F. A plant can survive a Georgia winter and still fail in a Georgia summer.
  • Rainfall and humidity. A plant that thrives in dry Colorado may rot in wet Florida at the same zone.
  • Snow cover. Snow insulates roots. A zone 4 yard with reliable snow may be gentler than a zone 6 yard with bare, windy winters.
  • Soil drainage, wind and exposure. Wet winter soil kills many plants that tolerate cold when dry.
  • Sharp, unusual cold snaps. The map is an average of annual lows, not a record. Any given year can be colder than your zone.

What is a microclimate and how can it change your zone?

A microclimate is a small area that is warmer or colder than your general zone. A sunny spot against a south-facing wall, a courtyard or a paved area can be a half zone to a full zone warmer, so you can sometimes grow a plant rated for a milder zone. A low hollow where cold air settles, or an exposed hilltop, can be a half zone to a full zone colder.

Use microclimates deliberately: put marginal plants in your warmest, most sheltered spot, and put hardy plants in the exposed areas. Watch your yard on a frosty morning to see where the frost melts first.

Don't "push" the zone with expensive plants. Buying a plant rated one or two zones too tender is a gamble. If you want to try, choose a smaller, cheaper plant, plant it in your warmest microclimate, mulch the roots in late fall, and accept that it may not return.

Why do hardiness zones matter for vegetable gardeners?

Strictly, hardiness zones matter most for perennials, trees, shrubs and overwintering crops such as garlic, asparagus and rhubarb. For annual vegetables, your last and first frost dates and your summer heat matter more. Your zone is still a handy shorthand: cooler zones (3–5) have short seasons and favor quick, cold-tolerant crops, while hot zones (9–10) shift cool-season crops like lettuce and broccoli to fall and winter.

Frequently asked questions

What hardiness zone am I in?

Enter your ZIP code on the USDA Plant Hardiness Zone Map website. It returns your zone, half zone and the temperature range for your area.

Did the USDA zones change in 2023?

Yes. The 2023 map, based on 1991–2020 weather data, moved about half the country into a warmer half zone compared with the 2012 map. Your plants don't change, but the average winter low in your area did.

Can I grow a plant that is rated for a warmer zone?

Sometimes, in a warm microclimate with good mulch and drainage. The plant might survive mild winters but die in a harsh one, so treat it as a risk, not a sure thing.

Is a lower zone number colder or warmer?

A lower number means colder winters. Zone 3 averages winter lows of −40°F to −30°F, while zone 10 averages 30°F to 40°F.

What is the difference between hardiness zones and frost dates?

Hardiness zones describe how cold winters get. Frost dates describe when the last spring and first fall freezes usually happen. Two areas in the same zone can have very different frost dates.

Do hardiness zones apply outside the United States?

The USDA map covers the US and Puerto Rico. Canada publishes its own plant hardiness zones and Europe uses separate systems, so use the map made for your region.

The bottom line

USDA hardiness zones tell you how cold winters usually get, in 10°F bands, so you can pick perennials, shrubs and trees that survive. They don't tell you frost dates, heat or rainfall, so use them together with a local frost date and your own microclimate.
