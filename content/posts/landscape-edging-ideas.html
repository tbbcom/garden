Quick answer

The cheapest landscape edging is a spade-cut trench; the most durable are steel, brick, stone and concrete; plastic sits between for cost and ease. Pick by how long you want it to last and how much effort you'll put in. Dig the trench 4–6 inches deep, and set rigid edging so it holds in your mulch depth.

Good edging does three jobs at once. It keeps mulch and gravel inside the bed, keeps lawn grass from creeping in and gives the yard a finished look that's out of proportion to what it costs.

This guide compares the main materials by cost, effort and where they work best, then shows how to install them and how much mulch the bed inside needs. To size the fill, use the mulch calculator.

Key takeaways
  • Free option: a spade-cut trench costs nothing but needs re-cutting once or twice a year.
  • Lasts longest: steel, brick, stone and concrete; plastic is the shortest-lived and cheapest.
  • Height: the edging should be at least as tall as your mulch layer so mulch doesn't spill over.
  • Curves: steel, plastic and small pavers follow curves; large blocks and poured concrete suit straight or gentle lines.
  • Mowing: set edging flush with or slightly below the lawn to run a mower wheel along it.

What is the cheapest way to edge a garden bed?

A spade-cut edge costs nothing. With a flat-bladed spade, cut a clean line along the bed and dig a V-shaped trench about 4–6 inches deep. Pull the sod out of the bed side, angle the lawn wall at roughly 45 degrees and fill the trench with mulch.

The trade-off is upkeep. Grass roots creep back, so plan to re-cut the edge once or twice a growing season. Spade-cut edging works best for informal beds and for people who like the soft, natural look.

Which edging lasts the longest?

Hard materials that don't rot or flex last the longest. Steel and aluminum resist heaving and bend into curves, but sharp tops can be a hazard, so choose rolled or capped tops where kids play. Brick, pavers and stone can last decades if set on a compacted base. Poured concrete lasts longest but is hard to change later.

Plastic and thin wood timbers are the least durable. Plastic can bow in heat and break from mower strikes, and untreated wood rots when it's in constant contact with soil.

Which edging is best for curves?

  • Steel and aluminum strips: bend by hand into gentle curves, held by stakes.
  • Plastic edging: flexible, but kinks on tight curves and needs frequent stakes.
  • Bricks or small pavers: follow tight curves because each unit is small.
  • Large stone blocks and poured concrete: best on straight lines or sweeping, gentle curves.

How do you install landscape edging?

  1. Mark the line. Lay a garden hose or rope along the curve and adjust it until it looks right from the street and from windows. Spray-paint the line.
  2. Cut or dig a trench. For rigid edging, dig a trench the width of the product and deep enough that the top sits where you want it, usually flush with the lawn or 1/2 inch above it.
  3. Set the edging. Place it in the trench, check the height along its length with a string line and backfill with soil, tamping as you go.
  4. Secure it. Drive stakes or spikes for metal and plastic. Set brick and stone in a base of compacted gravel or sand for stability.
  5. Add mulch. Fill the bed to 2–3 inches, leaving a few inches of bare soil around any trunk.

How tall should landscape edging be?

For holding in mulch, the top of the edging should rise at least as high as the mulch, so it contains a 3 inch layer without it spilling over. Most beds use edging that stands 2–4 inches above grade; steel strips are often 4–5 inches tall with 1–2 inches showing and the rest buried.

If you want to mow right up to the bed, set the top of the edge flush with the lawn or just below it. A raised edge needs separate string-trimming along it.

How much mulch does an edged bed need?

A bed with 100 ft of edging that's 4 ft wide is 400 sq ft. At 3 inches deep, that needs 400 × 3 ÷ 324 = 3.7 cubic yards, or about 50 bags of 2 cu ft. Measure inside the edging and use the mulch calculator for odd shapes.

Check before you dig. Call 811 before digging deeper than a few inches, and avoid trenching near tree roots. Cutting large roots near a trunk can damage or destabilize the tree, so route edging around them.

What are the most common edging mistakes?

  1. Setting it too low. Mulch spills over a 1 inch lip as soon as it rains.
  2. Skipping the base for brick and stone. Unsupported units tilt and sink after the first winter.
  3. Tight curves in stiff material. Plastic kinks and large blocks gap; choose a flexible or small-unit edge instead.
  4. Unfinished joins. Gaps at metal and plastic joints let grass roots through.
  5. No edge along walkways. Without a border, mulch and soil spread onto paths and drives.

Which edging suits which yard?

Match the material to the style of the house and the way you maintain the yard. A cottage-style border of mixed perennials looks right with a spade-cut edge or low stone. A modern front yard with ornamental grasses and gravel suits thin steel. A formal brick walk calls for matching brick or paver edging.

  • Mowing along it: choose flush edging, such as a mowing strip of pavers, so wheels roll over it.
  • Pets and kids: avoid sharp, thin metal tops; choose rolled-edge metal, brick or stone.
  • Rocky or rooty soil: a spade-cut edge or surface-laid stone is easier than trenching for steel.
  • Slopes: heavier stone or brick edging holds mulch back better than light plastic.

When in doubt, start with a spade-cut edge for a season. You'll learn where mulch tends to spill and where the lawn creeps in, then choose a permanent edging for those spots.

Frequently asked questions

What is the easiest landscape edging to install?

A spade-cut trench is the easiest: you only need a flat spade. Among products, roll-out plastic edging is the quickest to install, though it doesn't last as long as steel or stone.

How deep should you bury landscape edging?

Bury rigid edging so only 1–2 inches show above ground, with the rest below, so it stays put and resists heaving. For a spade-cut trench, dig a V-shape 4–6 inches deep.

Does steel edging rust?

Plain steel will rust, and some people like the rusty look. Galvanized or powder-coated steel and aluminum resist rust for much longer, so choose those if you want a clean finish.

Should I put landscape fabric under edging?

You don't need fabric under the edging itself. Brick and stone edging benefits from a compacted gravel or sand base for stability instead.

Can I use landscape edging to separate gravel from lawn?

Yes. Steel and aluminum edging give a clean, long-lasting boundary because they hold gravel in and keep grass roots out, and they bend well around curves.

The bottom line

Choose edging by lifespan and effort: a free spade-cut trench for informal beds, steel or aluminum for crisp lines, brick or stone for a traditional look and concrete for permanent runs. Set it at least as tall as your mulch and use the mulch calculator to fill the bed.
