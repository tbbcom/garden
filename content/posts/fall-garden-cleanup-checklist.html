Quick answer

Fall garden cleanup is mostly about removing diseased plants and weeds, not tidying everything. Pull spent vegetables, cut back diseased perennial foliage, plant bulbs and garlic, then leave hollow stems and seed heads standing until spring. Add 2–3 inches of shredded leaves or mulch after the ground starts to cool.

A perfectly bare garden looks neat in November but it isn't what plants, soil or wildlife need. The goal of fall cleanup is to break disease cycles, protect the soil and make spring easier, and you can do all of that in a weekend or two without bagging every leaf.

This checklist is ordered the way the work actually flows: vegetables first, then perennials, then leaves and mulch, then tools and water. If you want to know how much mulch your beds will take, the free mulch calculator turns bed sizes into cubic yards and bags.

Key takeaways
  • Always remove: diseased vegetable plants, weeds that have gone to seed and fruit left on the ground.
  • Often leave: hollow perennial stems and seed heads, which shelter native bees and feed birds until spring.
  • Mulch depth: 2–3 inches of shredded leaves or bark; 200 sq ft at 3 inches takes 50 cubic feet, about 12 bags of 30-gallon leaf bags.
  • Timing: plant bulbs and garlic before the ground freezes, and mulch perennials after it has cooled, not while it is still warm.
  • Tools and water: drain hoses and clean tools before the first hard freeze.

What should you do first in fall garden cleanup?

Start where cleanup matters most for plant health: the vegetable garden. Any plant that was sick this summer can carry its problem into next year, so these jobs come before the pretty tidy-up.

  1. Pull spent annual vegetables once frost has ended production, or sooner if the plants are finished.
  2. Bag or trash diseased plants. Tomato and potato foliage with blight, squash leaves with severe powdery mildew and any plant with a known soilborne disease should not go into a cool home compost pile.
  3. Pick up fallen fruit and tomatoes. They harbor rot and attract rodents.
  4. Pull weeds before they set seed, or cut off the seed heads and bag them.
  5. Leave roots of beans and peas in the ground and cut the tops instead; the roots break down and leave nitrogen-rich nodules in the soil.

What should you cut back and what should you leave?

This is the question behind most "fall cleanup" searches. A good rule: cut back what is diseased or mushy, leave what is hollow, sturdy or seed-bearing. The table below is a starting point; plants vary, so check your own.

Why leave hollow stems for pollinators?

Many native bees nest in hollow or pithy stems, and others overwinter in leaf litter. Extension programs and conservation groups such as the Xerces Society recommend leaving some stems and leaves in place, and cutting the stems you do remove to about 8–24 inches rather than flat to the ground. In spring, wait until daytime temperatures are regularly above about 50°F before cutting them down.

Don't compost diseased material in a cool pile. Home piles rarely stay hot enough to kill blight, mildew or soilborne fungi. Put diseased plants in the trash or yard-waste collection if your city allows it.

What do you do with fallen leaves?

Leaves are free mulch and compost, so the question is how much to keep. Thick whole leaves mat down and smother lawn and low plants, but shredded leaves break down fast and feed worms and soil life.

  • On the lawn: mow over them a few times so the pieces are dime-sized or smaller and fall down between the grass blades. If you can no longer see grass through the layer, rake or bag the excess.
  • In garden beds: shred and spread 2–3 inches between plants. Pull the layer back about 2 inches from stems and crowns.
  • In a compost pile: layer them with green material such as kitchen scraps and grass clippings.
  • As a pile of leaf mold: stack moistened leaves in a wire bin or bag for a year or two to make a soil conditioner.

A standard 30-gallon leaf bag holds about 4 cubic feet. To mulch 200 sq ft of beds at 3 inches you need 200 × 3 ÷ 12 = 50 cubic feet, or about 12 full bags of shredded leaves. Unshredded leaves take far more room, which is one reason shredding pays off.

When should you mulch perennials and shrubs?

Apply winter mulch after the soil has cooled and the first hard frosts have passed, not while it is still warm. The goal is to keep soil temperature steady and prevent freeze-thaw heaving, not to keep it warmer. In the coldest zones (3–5) that is often November; in zones 7–8 you may mulch mostly to protect shallow roots and suppress winter weeds.

Keep mulch a few inches away from trunks and stems. Piled-up mulch holds moisture against bark and gives mice a sheltered place to nibble.

What should you plant or divide in fall?

Fall is planting season for many things. Roots keep growing in cool soil even after the top of the plant slows down.

  • Spring bulbs: tulips, daffodils and crocuses go in after soil temperatures drop below about 60°F, and several weeks before the ground freezes.
  • Garlic: plant a few weeks before the ground freezes; our guide to planting dates by ZIP code helps with the timing.
  • Perennials, shrubs and trees: warm soil plus cool air is ideal. Water them well until the ground freezes.
  • Divide crowded perennials such as daylilies and hostas about 4–6 weeks before hard freeze so roots can settle.

How do you prep tools, hoses and irrigation?

Do this before the first hard freeze, which is usually around the average first frost date for your area. You can look up local dates with our frost dates by ZIP code tool.

  1. Drain and coil hoses, and store them out of the sun. Water left inside can freeze and split them.
  2. Disconnect hoses from spigots. A connected hose can trap water in the faucet and cause a pipe to burst.
  3. Blow out or drain in-ground sprinklers or hire someone to do it; check your system manual for the right air pressure.
  4. Clean and oil tools so rust does not set in over winter.
  5. Empty and store pots. Terra-cotta cracks when it freezes with wet soil inside.

5 fall cleanup mistakes to avoid

  1. Cutting everything to the ground. You remove overwintering habitat and seed for birds, and you don't gain much.
  2. Mulching too early. If the soil is still warm, mulch can keep rodents and slugs cozy and hold excess moisture against crowns.
  3. Composting diseased plants. This brings blight and mildew back next summer.
  4. Bagging all your leaves. You then buy mulch in spring that you already had for free.
  5. Tilling the whole garden. Digging in fall breaks soil structure and exposes bare soil to winter rain; cover it with mulch or a cover crop instead.

How long does fall cleanup take?

For an average suburban yard, plan roughly two weekends. Spend the first on vegetables, weeds and diseased foliage, and the second on leaves, mulch, bulbs and tools. Splitting the work this way keeps it from feeling like a chore, and it lets you wait for the right moment to cut back perennials and mulch rather than doing everything in one rush.

  • Weekend one (October): pull spent vegetables, remove diseased plants, plant garlic and bulbs, start a leaf pile.
  • Weekend two (after the first hard frosts): cut back collapsed perennials, mulch beds, drain hoses, clean and store tools.
  • Late winter: cut down the stems you left standing, once insects start to wake.

Where your first frost falls decides the whole schedule, so check the date for your ZIP code and work backward from it.

Frequently asked questions

When should I start fall garden cleanup?

Start after the first hard frost has killed tender annuals and vegetables, which is typically October to early November in much of the US. Do not rush perennials: they need foliage to store energy until it dies back on its own.

Should I cut back perennials in fall or spring?

Cut back plants with diseased or mushy foliage, such as peonies, hostas and bearded iris, in fall. Leave sturdy, hollow or seed-bearing stems such as coneflower and ornamental grasses until early spring.

Is it OK to leave leaves on garden beds over winter?

Yes, a thin layer of shredded leaves is good for beds. Avoid thick mats of whole leaves, especially oak or magnolia, which can smother low plants and shed water.

What vegetables can stay in the garden over winter?

Carrots, parsnips, leeks, kale and spinach tolerate frost and, with mulch, can be harvested well into winter in many areas. Garlic planted in fall also stays in the ground.

Should I water the garden in fall?

Yes, if the weather is dry. Evergreens, new trees and shrubs benefit from a deep soak until the ground freezes, because they continue to lose water through foliage in winter.

The bottom line

Remove what is diseased, leave what helps wildlife, use your leaves as mulch and get bulbs, garlic and tools sorted before the ground freezes. When you know how much cover your beds need, the mulch calculator turns the plan into a shopping list.
