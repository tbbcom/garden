Quick answer

To grow bell peppers, transplant them 2–3 weeks after your last frost, once soil is at least 65°F (18°C) and nights stay above 50°F (10°C). Give them 8+ hours of direct sun, rich soil with pH 6.0–6.8, 18–24 inches of spacing and 1–2 inches of water a week, applied evenly. Expect green peppers 60–90 days after transplanting.

Growing crisp, sweet bell peppers is a gardening rite of passage, and one of the most common sources of frustration. You provide sun, water and good soil, then get a few stunted fruits or a big green plant with nothing on it.

Bell peppers are far fussier than tomatoes. Nearly every failure traces back to three things: planting too early, uneven watering and the wrong fertilizer at the wrong time. This guide shows you how to get each one right, then how to diagnose and fix the problems that still show up. To find your exact planting window, use the planting calendar by ZIP code.

Key takeaways
  • Plant timing: 2–3 weeks after the last frost, soil ≥ 65°F (18°C), nights ≥ 50°F (10°C).
  • Sun: 8+ hours of direct sun for the best yield; 6 hours is the bare minimum.
  • Water: 1–2 inches (2.5–5 cm) a week, evenly. Wet–dry swings cause blossom end rot.
  • Feed: balanced or low-nitrogen fertilizer at planting, then every 2–3 weeks from first fruit set.
  • Harvest: 60–90 days from transplant; cut peppers off with shears, never pull.

Why do bell pepper plants fail?

Most gardeners treat peppers like tomatoes: same planting day, same watering, same fertilizer. Peppers are less forgiving. Their three classic problems (flowers falling off, no fruit forming and blossom end rot) are almost always caused by cold soil, temperature stress, too much nitrogen or inconsistent moisture. All four are preventable from day one.

When should you plant bell peppers?

Do not plant just because the garden center has transplants in stock. Planting too early is the number one mistake. Wait until 2–3 weeks after your average last frost date, and check the soil, not the air.

Pro tip: a soil thermometer costs a few dollars. Test 4 inches deep in the morning, and plant only when it reads 65°F (18°C) or warmer for several days in a row. Peppers set into 55°F soil can sit stunted for weeks and often never catch up with later plantings.

What tools and materials do you need?

Gather everything before planting day so transplants don't sit drying out in their pots.

  • Healthy transplants: 4–6 inches tall, sturdy stems, dark green leaves. Skip plants already flowering or fruiting.
  • Compost: the single best soil amendment for peppers.
  • Granular vegetable fertilizer: a balanced 10-10-10 or a lower-nitrogen 5-10-10 for mixing into the soil.
  • Liquid fertilizer: fish emulsion or a tomato and vegetable feed for side-dressing.
  • Soil thermometer, trowel, watering can or hose.
  • Organic mulch: straw, shredded leaves or pine bark fines.
  • Stakes or small cages for support later in the season.
  • For containers: a pot of at least 5 gallons (12–14 in / 30–36 cm across) per plant, with large drainage holes.
Safety first: wear gardening gloves when handling compost, manure and soil, especially with cuts on your hands, and wash your hands with soap and water before eating. Follow the label exactly for any fertilizer, insecticidal soap or neem product.

How do you plant bell peppers step by step?

These five steps set up the whole season. Don't rush them.

Step 1: Pick the sunniest spot

For bell peppers there is almost no such thing as too much sun. Aim for all-day sun. In very hot climates (the Deep South, desert Southwest), a spot with full morning sun and light late-afternoon shade reduces sunscald and blossom drop.

Step 2: Prepare the bed or container

  • In the ground: loosen the soil 8–12 inches (20–30 cm) deep and mix in 2–3 inches (5–8 cm) of compost or well-rotted manure. Work in granular fertilizer at the rate on the package. A soil test tells you whether you need lime or sulfur to reach pH 6.0–6.8.
  • In containers: never use garden soil; it compacts and drowns roots. Use bagged potting mix blended about 3:1 with compost for a rich, free-draining mix. The soil calculator tells you how many bags fill your pots or raised bed.

Step 3: Harden off your transplants (the step most people skip)

Greenhouse-grown transplants have never felt wind or full sun. Planting them straight out causes leaf scorch and transplant shock. Toughen them up over 7–10 days:

  1. Days 1–2: 2–3 hours outdoors in a sheltered, shady spot. Bring them in at night.
  2. Days 3–4: 3–4 hours of dappled or morning sun.
  3. Days 5–6: 5–6 hours of direct sun.
  4. Days 7–10: outdoors full-time in their final spot, coming in only if nights drop below 50°F (10°C).

Step 4: Plant at the same depth, 18–24 inches apart

Dig a hole as deep as the pot and slightly wider. Squeeze the pot to loosen the root ball and slide the plant out.

Unlike tomatoes, peppers should not be planted deeper. Set the plant at the same soil level it grew at in its pot, as illustrated below. Burying the stem can lead to stem rot.

Space plants 18–24 inches (45–60 cm) apart in rows 24–36 inches apart. In a 4 × 8 ft raised bed that's about 8 plants. Use the plant spacing calculator for any other bed size.

Step 5: Water deeply, then mulch

Soak the root zone right after planting to settle the soil. Then spread 2–3 inches of organic mulch around each plant, keeping it an inch or two away from the stem. Mulch is the quiet secret of good peppers: it holds moisture steady, smothers weeds and keeps root temperatures even. The mulch calculator works out how many bags your beds need.

How often should you water bell peppers?

Bell peppers need about 1–2 inches of water a week, from rain plus irrigation, and they need it consistently. Letting soil go bone-dry and then flooding it is the main cause of blossom end rot, because the stress interrupts calcium moving into the young fruit.

  • The goal: soil as moist as a wrung-out sponge, never soggy and never cracked dry.
  • The test: push a finger 2 inches into the soil. If it's dry at that depth, water deeply.
  • In-ground beds: usually a deep soak 2–3 times a week, more in heat waves.
  • Containers: often daily in hot weather, until water runs from the drainage holes.
  • Best method: drip line or soaker hose at the base, in the morning. Wet leaves invite disease.

Should you pinch or prune pepper plants?

Pinch the first flower

When the plant is about 8–10 inches tall, its main stem splits into a "Y", and a single flower bud (the crown flower) forms in the fork. Pinch it off. It feels wrong, but it redirects the plant's energy into roots and branches instead of one early fruit, and more branches means more flowers later. Remove any flowers that form in the first 2–3 weeks after transplanting, too.

Don't over-prune

Leave the leaf canopy alone. Leaves shade the fruit and prevent sunscald. Only remove yellowing, diseased or damaged leaves.

Stake before the fruit gets heavy

Pepper stems are brittle, and a branch loaded with fruit can snap in wind or rain. When the plant is about a foot tall, push a sturdy stake 2–3 inches from the stem and tie the plant loosely with soft twine, or use a small tomato cage.

Plant is big but has no flowers or fruit

Cause: too much nitrogen (lawn food or a high-nitrogen all-purpose feed), or fewer than 6 hours of sun. Fix: stop all fertilizing, keep watering evenly and give it time. Switch to a low-nitrogen feed only once flowers appear.

Flowers bloom, then fall off (blossom drop)

Cause: temperature stress. Peppers abort flowers when nights fall below about 55–60°F (13–16°C), when nights stay above 75°F (24°C) or when days stay above 90°F (32°C). Drought and very low or very high humidity make it worse. Fix: you can't control the weather, but mulch and steady watering reduce the stress. Plants start setting fruit again when temperatures moderate.

Black or brown rotten spot on the bottom of the fruit (blossom end rot)

This is the most infamous pepper problem. It looks like a disease but is a calcium delivery problem: a dark, sunken, leathery patch on the blossom end of the fruit, as illustrated below.

  • Cause: not enough calcium reaching the developing fruit. In most gardens the soil has enough calcium; uneven watering (dry spells followed by soaking) interrupts its uptake. Heavy nitrogen feeding and root damage from deep hoeing add to it.
  • Fix: don't reach for lime or calcium sprays first. Fix the watering: water deeply and evenly and keep the mulch in place. Pick off affected fruit; new fruit usually develops normally. Add lime only if a soil test shows low pH or low calcium.

University extension services, including the University of Minnesota Extension, describe blossom end rot the same way: a calcium-uptake disorder in the fruit that is best prevented with steady soil moisture and mulch, not with extra calcium.

White, papery spots on the skin (sunscald)

Cause: the pepper version of sunburn, on fruit exposed to intense direct sun. Fix: the fruit is still edible once the damaged patch is cut off. Prevent it with a bushy, unpruned plant, or 30% shade cloth in the afternoon during heat waves.

Tiny insects on new leaves (aphids)

Cause: aphids love tender new growth. Fix: knock them off with a strong spray of water. For persistent colonies, spray leaf undersides with insecticidal soap or neem oil, following the label, in the cool of the evening.

When and how do you harvest bell peppers?

Green vs colored: the ripeness test

A green bell pepper is simply an unripe pepper. You can pick at any stage, but fruit left on the plant turns red, yellow or orange (depending on variety), gets sweeter and gains vitamin C. According to USDA FoodData Central, raw red bell peppers contain about 128 mg of vitamin C per 100 g, compared with about 80 mg for green.

  • Green peppers: pick when full-size, firm and glossy, usually 60–75 days after transplanting.
  • Colored peppers: pick once they reach their full, deep color, typically 2–3 weeks later.
Yield tip: picking some peppers green signals the plant to set more flowers. For the most peppers, pick green regularly. For the sweetest, most nutritious peppers, let them ripen on the plant.

Cut, don't pull

Never pull a pepper off by hand. The branches are brittle and you can tear off a whole branch, losing future fruit. Use clean pruning shears or a sharp knife and cut the stem about half an inch (1 cm) above the pepper, as illustrated below.

Harvest everything, green or not, before the first fall frost. Even a light frost ruins the fruit.

5 bell pepper mistakes to avoid

  1. Planting on the tomato schedule. Peppers need warmer soil; wait the extra 2–3 weeks.
  2. Burying the stem. Plant at pot depth, not deep like tomatoes.
  3. Watering in feast-or-famine cycles. This is the root of blossom end rot.
  4. Feeding high-nitrogen fertilizer. You get leaves, not peppers.
  5. Pulling fruit off by hand. Cut it, or lose the branch.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to grow bell peppers?

Bell peppers take about 60–90 days from transplanting to harvest. Green peppers are usually ready in 60–75 days, and fully colored red, yellow or orange peppers take 2–3 weeks longer. From seed, add another 8–10 weeks of indoor growing.

How many hours of sun do bell peppers need?

Bell peppers need at least 6 hours of direct sun a day, and 8 or more hours gives the best yield. With less sun, plants grow leggy and produce few or no flowers.

How many peppers does one bell pepper plant produce?

A healthy bell pepper plant typically produces about 5–10 full-size peppers per season, more in long, warm seasons and fewer when fruit is left to ripen to full color. Picking some fruit green encourages the plant to set more.

Can I grow bell peppers in a 5-gallon bucket?

Yes. A 5-gallon (about 19 L) pot or bucket is the minimum size for one bell pepper plant. Drill several large drainage holes in the bottom, fill it with potting mix blended with compost, and expect to water daily in hot weather.

Do bell peppers come back every year?

Bell peppers are perennials in frost-free climates but are grown as annuals in most of the US because frost kills them. In zones 10–11 a plant can keep producing for a second year, and gardeners in colder zones can overwinter one in a pot indoors, though yields are usually lower.

Why are my bell peppers small?

Small peppers usually come from too little water during fruit growth, too little sun, an overcrowded plant or a pot that is too small. Even watering, 18–24 inches of spacing and a feed every 2–3 weeks from first fruit set help fruit size up.

The bottom line

To grow bell peppers that actually produce, plant them 2–3 weeks after your last frost into soil at 65°F or warmer, give them 8+ hours of sun, water 1–2 inches a week without wet–dry swings, and feed lightly from first fruit set. Pinch the first flower, stake early and cut, never pull, at harvest. Check your exact transplant window in the free planting calendar.
